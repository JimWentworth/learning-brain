The Prefrontal Cortex The mind’s workbench

Behind your forehead, the prefrontal cortex holds what you are thinking about and keeps you on task. Its workbench is small, and knowing that changes how to learn.

The prefrontal cortex is the front part of the brain’s frontal lobe, just behind the forehead. In 1989, Shintaro Funahashi, Charles Bruce and Patricia Goldman-Rakic at Yale recorded single cells there while monkeys did a simple memory task. A spot of light flashed somewhere on a screen and went out. After a wait of a few seconds, the monkey had to look at where the spot had been.

Some cells fired while the light was on. Others did something new: they kept firing through the wait, after the light was gone. Many of them fired only for spots in one direction. The cells were holding the location in mind until it was needed.

Working memoryA small workbench

Psychologists call this working memory: holding information in mind while you use it. Following directions, comparing two prices or working out a sum in your head all depend on it. Long-term memories are stored elsewhere, with help from the hippocampus. The prefrontal cortex is less a filing cabinet than a workbench, where a few things stay active while you work with them.

The workbench is small. In 1956 George Miller famously suggested people can hold about seven items. When researchers stop people from grouping items, the number is lower. In a 2001 review, Nelson Cowan put the limit at about three to five chunks, with four as a typical figure.

A chunk is a group you already know as one unit. In 1973, William Chase and Herbert Simon showed chess players a board for five seconds and asked them to rebuild it. A master rebuilt positions from real games far better than a beginner. With the pieces placed at random, most of the master’s advantage disappeared. The master did not have a bigger workbench. He saw familiar groups of pieces, so each chunk carried several pieces at once.

Self-controlDoing what you mean to do

The prefrontal cortex also keeps you on task, by holding your goal in mind and holding back habits that get in the way. In 1935, John Ridley Stroop asked people to name the ink color of printed words. When the word named a different color, such as RED printed in blue ink, people were slower and made more mistakes. Reading is so practised that it happens automatically. Naming the ink means overriding it.

Last to matureA region that keeps developing

The prefrontal cortex is one of the last brain regions to mature. In a study published in 2004, Nitin Gogtay, Paul Thompson and colleagues scanned the same 13 children every two years for eight to ten years, between the ages of 4 and 21. Areas for basic senses and movement matured first. Regions that combine information, including the prefrontal cortex, matured last, into young adulthood.

The region most sensitive to stress

Amy Arnsten’s research at Yale shows that the prefrontal cortex is especially sensitive to stress. Even mild stress, when it feels out of your control, can quickly weaken working memory. Longer stress can change the shape of prefrontal cells. The same brain chemicals that weaken the prefrontal cortex strengthen the amygdala, which drives emotional reactions.

For learning, this is one more reason to keep practice low-stakes. A calmer learner has more workbench to work with.

Why it matters for learningDon’t overload the workbench

  1. Write it downNotes, lists and sketches hold what your workbench doesn’t need to. Save your working memory for the thinking.
  2. One new step at a timeBreak new material into small steps, and study a worked example before solving problems alone.
  3. Practise until it’s automaticOnce the basics become chunks, they take up less space, leaving room for the new and harder parts.
