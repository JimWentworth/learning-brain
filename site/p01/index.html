Perception is a best guess

Your eyes send the brain patchy, ambiguous signals. What you see is the brain’s best interpretation of them, built from context and past experience. Usually it’s right. It’s also why a student and an expert can look at the same diagram and see different things.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Project the student version full screen. Ask everyone to decide privately which square, A or B, is lighter. Then take a show of hands.
  2. Press “Cover the scene” and vote again. With the board and shadow covered, the two squares look the same.
  3. Show the bars that join A and B. Ask: now that you know, does B still look lighter in the full picture?
  4. In pairs, students explain what their brain assumed about the light and the shadow.
  5. Bridge to your subject: where might a beginner “see” something different from what you see?

Ask the room

  • Why might a visual system that gets this “wrong” still be a good design?
  • What did your brain assume about the shadow?
  • Where in this course might a beginner see something different from what an expert sees?

Connect it to your course

Before you show a diagram, graph, specimen or text, say what to look at and what to ignore. Your eye already knows what matters. Your students’ eyes are still guessing.

Good to know

Projectors change contrast. Check the effect on your room’s projector beforehand; if it washes out, have students use their own screens.

More than 150 years ago, Hermann von Helmholtz described what we see as “unconscious inferences”: conclusions the brain draws from sensory data and past knowledge. The psychologist Richard Gregory later compared perceptions to the hypotheses of science: predictions about what is out there.

The checker-shadow picture, published by Edward Adelson at MIT in 1995, shows this at work. Squares A and B are the same grey. Your brain takes the shadow into account and reports what the surfaces are probably like, not how much light reaches your eye.

Why guess?The signal is ambiguous

The same pattern on the retina could come from many different scenes: a dark square in bright light, or a light square in shadow. The brain can’t measure which. It settles the question with context and with what usually happens in the world.

Knowledge winsThe hollow face

Look at the inside of a hollow mask and you will usually see a normal face bulging toward you. Gregory called this evidence for the power of top-down knowledge in vision: we have seen so many faces that bulge out that this expectation overrides shading and even the depth cues from two eyes. The illusion is strongest right side up and weakest upside down, where our experience of faces helps least.

A cautionIllusions are not failures

The same machinery that makes square B look lighter is what lets you recognise a white page in candlelight and in sunshine. Illusions are cases where a usually good guess goes wrong. They let us see the guessing.

For learningExperts and beginners guess differently

If perception depends on what you already know, then a beginner and an expert don’t see the same diagram. The expert’s brain fills in structure the beginner can’t yet see. Later topics in this series look at how that changes with practice, and at how to point students to what matters.
