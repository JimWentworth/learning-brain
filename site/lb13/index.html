A monkey gets an unexpected sip of juice, and dopamine neurons in its midbrain fire a burst. Once a light reliably comes before the juice, the burst moves to the light. And if the light comes but the juice doesn’t, the neurons dip below their usual rate, right when the juice was due.

Wolfram Schultz and colleagues showed that these neurons are not simply signalling pleasure. They track the difference between what was expected and what arrived: a reward prediction error.

The signalBetter, as expected, worse

Better than expected brings a burst; just as expected, nothing; worse than expected, a dip. It is the prediction signal from the page on prediction, tuned to what matters, and the reward system page looks at it in more detail.

CuriosityWanting to know

Curiosity recruits the same circuit. In 2014, Matthias Gruber, Bernard Gelman and Charan Ranganath had people read trivia questions, rate how curious they were, and wait a few seconds for each answer. The more curious they were, the more their midbrain reward areas and nucleus accumbens engaged, and the better they remembered the answers, both straight away and a day later. They even remembered better the unrelated faces shown during the wait.

RewardsPrizes and praise are not the same

External rewards are trickier. In 1999, Edward Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard Ryan pooled 128 experiments. When people expected a tangible reward, such as money or a prize, for doing an interesting task, they later chose to keep doing it on their own less often. Positive, informative feedback did the opposite: it raised both free-choice time on the task and reported interest.

RelevanceWhy should I care?

In 2009, Chris Hulleman and Judith Harackiewicz asked high-school science students to write, during the term, about how the material connected to their own lives. Students who had expected to do poorly gained interest in science and earned higher grades than similar students who wrote summaries instead. A later study replicated the result.

Some cautionRewards are not all bad

The undermining effect applies to tasks people already find interesting, and to rewards that are expected and tangible. Rewards for dull tasks, or unexpected ones, behave differently. And curiosity studies use trivia; a classroom question has to be one students actually want answered.

Show the gap before you fill it

Open with a question students want answered, and pause before the reveal. Give feedback that informs, about strategy and progress, rather than points for everything. Ask students to connect the material to their own goals, and let surprise do some of the work.
