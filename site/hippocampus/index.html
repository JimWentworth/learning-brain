In 1953, a surgeon in Hartford, Connecticut, operated on a 27-year-old man named Henry Molaison to stop his severe epileptic seizures. He removed tissue from deep inside both sides of the brain, including about two-thirds of the hippocampus on each side.

The seizures eased. But Molaison, known in research as H.M., could no longer form new memories of facts or events. He lived with that for the next 55 years. Tests he took part in showed what the hippocampus does, and his became one of the most studied cases in neuroscience.

        How it worksAn index for each memory

        The hippocampus is a curved strip of tissue deep inside each temporal lobe. The anatomist Julius Caesar Aranzi named it in 1587 for its shape: hippocampus is Greek for seahorse.

        When you live through an event, different parts of the cortex handle its sights, its sounds and the words you hear. The hippocampus links those parts into one memory. In the “indexing” view proposed by Timothy Teyler and Pascal DiScenna in 1986, it keeps something like an index that points to where each part is kept, so a single cue can bring the whole memory back.

        The cellular changeConnections that stay stronger

        In 1966, Terje Lømo was recording from the hippocampus of anaesthetised rabbits in Oslo. He noticed that a brief burst of signals made one set of connections pass on signals more strongly, and the change lasted for hours. With Timothy Bliss he described it in detail in 1973.

        The effect is now called long-term potentiation. It is one of the main ways scientists think connections between neurons store what we learn, and it was first found in the hippocampus.

        Over days and nightsFrom hippocampus to cortex

        A new memory doesn’t stay dependent on the hippocampus forever. Over the following days, weeks and longer, especially during sleep, it is replayed and gradually comes to rely more on networks in the cortex. Researchers call this systems consolidation.

        Why it matters for learningWhat the hippocampus needs

        1. AttentionIt can only link what you took in. Multitasking while studying leaves it less to work with.
        2. TimeSeveral short sessions on different days give it more chances to take in and link the material than one long session.
        3. SleepNew memories are replayed during sleep. Studying late and sleeping little cuts that short.
