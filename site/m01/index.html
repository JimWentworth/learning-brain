Picture a US penny. You have handled thousands. Which way does Lincoln face? What words are above his head? In a 1979 study by Raymond Nickerson and Marilyn Adams, most people could not pick the real penny out of 15 drawings.

Seeing something thousands of times is not the same as storing it. This series follows the life of a single memory, and this page starts with its three stages.

Three stagesTaken in, made to last, found again

First a memory is encoded: taken in and given a form. Then it is consolidated: stabilized so that it lasts. Finally it is retrieved: found again when it is needed. A memory can fail at any of the three, and each failure looks different.

The penny is an encoding failure. Nobody needs those details to spend a coin, so they were never really taken in. Repetition alone didn’t do it. What gets stored depends on what the learner does with the material, not on how often it passes in front of them.

In the brain, the hippocampus helps bind the parts of a new experience into a memory. The prefrontal cortex helps steer what gets processed and search for it later. In a 1998 brain-scanning study, Anthony Wagner and colleagues found that stronger activity in left prefrontal and temporal regions while people judged a word predicted whether they would remember it later.

ConsolidateMost of the loss comes early

Consolidation failure looks different. In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus learned lists of nonsense syllables, then measured how much faster he could relearn them later. After 20 minutes, he saved 58% of the original effort. After a day, about a third. After a month, about a fifth. Most of the loss came early, while the memory was still new. A 2015 replication by Jaap Murre and Joeri Dros found a very similar curve.

RetrieveStored the whole time

Retrieval failure looks different again. In a 1966 experiment by Endel Tulving and Zena Pearlstone, people studied word lists sorted into categories. Asked to recall freely, they missed many words. Given the category names as cues, they recalled far more. Most of the gain came from whole categories they had missed. The words were stored all along; they just couldn’t be reached without a way in.

One cautionA model, not three boxes

The three stages are a model, not three separate boxes. Retrieving a memory also changes it, and encoding builds on what is already stored. The stages overlap, and later topics in this series show where.

Diagnose the failure

Each case has one main failure.

“I studied, but I couldn’t remember”

When a student says this, ask which stage failed. Did they really work with the material the first time? Did they come back to it within days, or let it fade? Or do they know it, but can’t find it without the right cue? Each problem has a different fix: deeper work at the start, spaced return visits, or practice at retrieving with fewer and fewer cues.
