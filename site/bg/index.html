The Basal Ganglia Habits and skills

Deep in the brain, the basal ganglia learn slowly, by repetition. They turn practice into routines that run without thinking, which is a gift for learning and a trap for bad habits.

In 1996, Barbara Knowlton, Jennifer Mangels and Larry Squire gave people a game of weather forecasting. On each turn a few cards with shapes appeared, and players guessed rain or sun. Each card predicted the weather only some of the time, so there was no rule to memorise. The game could only be learned slowly, by playing.

People with amnesia, caused by damage to memory regions of the brain, learned the game as well as anyone, though they could barely remember the training. People with Parkinson’s disease did the opposite: they remembered the training sessions but did not learn the game. Learning a habit, it seemed, depends on a different part of the brain from remembering what happened.

Where they areA loop deep in the brain

The basal ganglia are a group of structures deep inside the brain, connected in loops with the cortex. Their largest part is the striatum, made up of the caudate nucleus and the putamen. Knowlton’s team concluded that the striatum is essential for the slow, step-by-step learning of associations that is typical of habits.

The striatum depends on dopamine, a chemical sent from cells in the midbrain, in a region called the substantia nigra. In Parkinson’s disease those cells die. By the time movement symptoms appear, most people have lost 60 to 80 percent or more of them. The best-known result is trouble starting and controlling movement, but the 1996 study showed that learning by repetition suffers too.

From steps to chunksHow a route becomes a habit

Ann Graybiel’s team at MIT recorded cells in the striatum while rats learned to run a maze for a reward. At first, cells fired throughout each run. As the run became a habit, the firing gathered at the beginning and the end, with little in between. The whole run had become one chunk, started and stopped as a single unit. The pattern stayed stable for weeks.

That is what a habit is: a routine triggered by a cue that runs with little thought. It frees the prefrontal workbench for other things. It also explains why habits are hard to break, even once they no longer help.

How long?Not 21 days

In a study published in 2010, Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London asked 96 volunteers to pick a new healthy behavior, such as eating a piece of fruit with lunch, and do it every day for 12 weeks. Each day they rated how automatic it felt.

30 days

Of the 82 people who finished, the data for 39 fitted a clear habit curve: big gains in automaticity at first, then a plateau. For them, the typical time to reach it was 66 days, but it ranged from 18 to 254 days, and some behaviors took much longer than others. Missing a single day did not significantly set people back. Being very inconsistent did.

Skills, not just habits

The same slow, repetition-driven learning builds skills such as reading words at a glance, typing and recalling multiplication facts. Once those basics run on their own, they take up little room on the prefrontal workbench, leaving space for the harder thinking a lesson is really about.

Why it matters for learningMake the basics automatic

  1. Repeat oftenShort, frequent practice of the basics beats an occasional long session. Missing a day is fine; being inconsistent is not.
  2. Same cue, same settingHabits form when the same cue leads to the same action. Tie a new routine to something you already do every day.
  3. Practise with feedbackRepetition makes whatever you practise automatic, mistakes included. Get the form right early.
