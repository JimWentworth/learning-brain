Values affirmation Promising ideas deserve testing, not faith

Can a brief writing exercise narrow an achievement gap? A 2006 study in Science said yes. Bigger, careful replications mostly did not. Here is what that means for your course.

The exercise is called values affirmation. Students choose values that matter to them, like family, friends, music or sport, and write briefly about why. The theory: reminding students of a larger sense of self buffers the threat of being judged through a stereotype.

In the original study, by Geoffrey Cohen and colleagues in 2006, seventh graders did the exercise early in the school year. For African American students, it reduced the racial achievement gap in the course by about 40%, and follow-ups reported benefits lasting two years.

Bigger testsMostly empty

Then came bigger tests. A study across the original district reported a smaller benefit. But a large, careful replication by Paul Hanselman and colleagues in the same district found no effect, and could rule out benefits larger than about 0.1. Other replications, including one by John Protzko and Joshua Aronson, and one in college physics, also came up empty.

So values affirmation is not a reliable fix. It may help in some settings, perhaps where threat is high and the exercise is delivered with care. But no one can yet predict where.

A cautionAn experiment, not a fix

The original results were striking, and the best replications did not find them. If you use the exercise, you are running an experiment, and it should not replace the structural work in the rest of this series.

For teaching

If you try it, treat it as an experiment. Keep it brief, private and ungraded, early in the term or before a high-stakes test. Track what happens. And don’t let it replace the structural work in the rest of this series.
