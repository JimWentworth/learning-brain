Attention A competition in the brain

Your eyes take in far more than your brain can fully process. Objects compete for the brain’s limited processing, and attention is a bias on that competition. Some things win because they stand out; others win because you chose them.

Imagine a desk with four objects on it. Light from all four reaches your eyes at the same moment, but your brain can’t fully process all of them at once. Something has to decide which gets through.

In 1995, Robert Desimone and John Duncan proposed an answer called biased competition. Objects in view compete for processing in the visual cortex. Attention doesn’t work like a spotlight that switches things on. It tips the balance of a competition that is already happening.

The competitionBigger windows, more crowding

Each cell in the visual cortex responds to one patch of the scene, its receptive field. In the first visual area, V1, those patches are tiny. Further along, they grow: in people, they have been estimated at under 2 degrees across in V1 and 4 to 6 degrees in V4, at a point a little way out from the centre of gaze. A bigger patch can hold several objects at once, and when it does, their signals don’t add up. They suppress each other.

In 1998, Sabine Kastner, Peter De Weerd, Robert Desimone and Leslie Ungerleider measured this in people with brain scans. They showed four colourful images either one after another or all at once. In V4 and beyond, the response to all four together was smaller than to the same images shown in turn. In V1, where each cell’s patch was too small to hold more than one image, there was little difference.

Bottom-upSome things win on their own

One way to win the competition is to stand out. Something brighter than its surroundings, or different from everything around it, captures attention automatically. Anne Treisman and Garry Gelade showed in 1980 that a target differing by one simple feature, such as colour, seems to pop out: it is found just as fast however many other items there are. A target defined by a combination of features takes longer with every item added.

Top-downChoosing the winner

The other way to win is to be chosen. Areas in the frontal and parietal lobes send signals back to the visual cortex, toward the cells covering the place or feature you are looking for. In Kastner’s experiment, when people attended to the location of the images, the response there grew, and much of the suppression between the images was cancelled out.

The bias can be set up before anything appears. In 1997, Steven Luck, Leonardo Chelazzi, Steven Hillyard and Robert Desimone recorded from monkeys waiting for a stimulus. When attention was directed to a cell’s patch, its spontaneous firing in areas V2 and V4 was 30 to 40% higher, even though the screen there was still blank.

This is Michael Posner’s cueing task from 1980. The arrow sets up a bias before the dot appears, and responses are faster at the expected place and slower at the other one, even though your eyes never move. It is the behavioural version of the bias Luck and colleagues saw in monkey cells.

Some cautionFrom the lab to the lecture

Most of this evidence comes from simple displays, brain scans and recordings in monkeys. How cleanly it carries over to a crowded lecture slide is an open question. Still, the logic is consistent: attention is a bias on a competition, and whatever wins is what gets passed on to memory and action.

Set the bias before the slide

Tell students what to look for before a slide or demonstration appears, so the bias is in place early. And cut what competes with the one thing that matters: decoration, extra text and animations all join the competition.

Why it matters for learningAttention is a bias, not a switch

  1. Say where to look, firstPoint to what matters before it appears, so attention is already biased toward it.
  2. Cut the competitionRemove decoration and extra text that compete with the key point.
  3. Make the key thing stand outA single feature that differs, such as colour, pops out without effort.
