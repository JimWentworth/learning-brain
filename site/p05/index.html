The brain corrects the light

The colour and lightness you see depend on what surrounds them and on what the brain assumes about the light. That is why one photo of a dress split the internet, and why a colour that works on your laptop can vanish on the projector.

Run it in class

  1. Project part 1. Ask a volunteer to drag the slider until the two squares look the same, while the class calls out “lighter” or “darker”.
  2. Press “Done”. The page shows the value they chose and joins the squares with a bar of the original grey.
  3. In part 2, the class votes on each colour pair: does it pass the 4.5 to 1 contrast standard? Reveal the ratio each time.
  4. Bring one of your own slides and check its colours the same way.

Ask the room

  • Why did the square on the dark background look lighter?
  • Which pairs surprised you?
  • How might a colour-coded chart look to someone with red–green colour vision deficiency?

Connect it to your course

Lightness and colour are judged relative to their surroundings, and projectors wash colours out. Check text contrast with a contrast checker rather than by eye, and never use colour as the only way to show a difference.

Good to know

Results vary with the screen, the room’s light and the viewer. That variation is part of the lesson.

In 2015, a photo of a striped dress divided the internet. Rosa Lafer-Sousa, Katherine Hermann and Bevil Conway surveyed 1,401 people: 57% saw it as blue and black, 30% as white and gold, and 11% as blue and brown.

Their explanation: the photo gives few clues about the lighting. People who assume a cool, bluish light, like daylight from a blue sky, discount the blue and see white and gold. People who assume warm, artificial light discount the yellow and see blue and black.

ConstancySeeing the surface, not the light

The light reaching your eye from a sheet of paper changes enormously between candlelight and noon. The paper still looks white. The brain estimates the lighting and discounts it, so that we see the surface, not the light. Usually that’s what we want. When the lighting is ambiguous, as in the dress photo, different brains can make different assumptions, and see different colours.

ContextA grey is lighter on dark

The same grey looks lighter on a dark background than on a light one, which is what you measured in part 1. In a chart, the same colour can look different depending on what sits next to it.

Not everyone sees it the sameColour vision differs

Red–green colour vision deficiency affects about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women among people of Northern European ancestry, and is less common in most other populations. A chart that relies on red versus green alone may carry no information for some of your students.

For learningCheck contrast, don’t eyeball it

The web accessibility guidelines (WCAG) ask for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text, and 3 to 1 for large text. Our judgement of contrast is relative, so a colour that looks fine on your bright laptop can fade on a projector. Use a contrast checker, and pair colour with labels, patterns or position.
