The Entorhinal Cortex The brain’s grid

Next to the hippocampus, cells fire in a repeating pattern of triangles as you move. They help you keep track of where you are, and they may help organize ideas too.

Place cells in the hippocampus each fire in one spot. In 2005, May-Britt and Edvard Moser and their team recorded from the entorhinal cortex, the region that feeds information into the hippocampus, and found something stranger. As a rat explored, one cell fired in many spots, and the spots formed a grid of triangles covering the whole floor.

Nothing on the floor was marked that way. The brain makes the grid itself. In 2014, the Mosers shared the Nobel Prize with John O’Keefe, who discovered place cells.

The teamA compass, an edge detector and a grid

Grid cells share the entorhinal cortex with other navigation cells. Head direction cells fire when your head points one way, like a compass needle. Border cells fire near walls and edges. Grid cells come in sets of different sizes, from fine to coarse, and in rats the grids get wider along the region.

Together these cells can keep track of where you are from your own movements: which way you turned and how far you went. This is called path integration, or dead reckoning. Its errors build up over time, and landmarks such as walls help correct them.

    In peopleA grid in the human brain

    In 2010, Christian Doeller, Caswell Barry and Neil Burgess scanned people as they moved through a virtual world. Activity in the entorhinal cortex rose and fell with the direction of travel, peaking every 60 degrees: the six-fold pattern grid cells produce. People with the clearest pattern were best at remembering where objects were.

    The entorhinal cortex is also one of the first regions affected by Alzheimer’s disease. Getting lost in a familiar place can be an early sign, and researchers are testing navigation tasks as early warning tools.

    The brain lays a grid over space, and maybe over ideas too.

    Beyond spaceA map for concepts

    In 2016, Alexandra Constantinescu, Jill O’Reilly and Timothy Behrens asked people to learn about cartoon birds whose necks and legs varied in length. As people thought about changes in the birds, their brains showed the same six-fold pattern seen during navigation. The researchers suggest the grid code may help organize knowledge laid out along two dimensions, though this idea is still being tested.

    Why it matters for learningMap your ideas

    1. Lay ideas outPlace related ideas on two dimensions, such as cost and benefit, or time and place, so you can see how they relate.
    2. Use landmarksAnchor a topic with a few key examples. Like landmarks on a walk, they correct errors that creep into what you remember.
    3. Travel between ideasPractise moving from one idea to another and explaining the route. Knowing the links is what makes a map useful.
