From fragile to stable A memory is still being built

New memories start fragile and settle with time: over minutes, over hours as synapses change, and over years as storage shifts from the hippocampus to the cortex.

In 1900, two German psychologists, Georg Elias Müller and Alfons Pilzecker, had people learn a list of nonsense-syllable pairs, then a second list. When the second list came right away, memory for the first suffered. When it came six minutes later, the first list held up better.

New memories, they concluded, need time to set. They called the process consolidation.

In the synapseMaking a change last

In 1973, Tim Bliss and Terje Lømo gave a pathway into the hippocampus of anaesthetized rabbits a brief burst of stimulation. Afterwards, the connection responded more strongly for periods of 30 minutes to 10 hours; in later work with awake rabbits, for up to 3 days. This is long-term potentiation.

Making changes like this last requires new proteins. Reviewing the evidence in 1984, Hasker Davis and Larry Squire concluded that blocking protein synthesis in animals around the time of learning left short-term memory intact, but long-term memory failed. The window is hours, not weeks.

The engramWhere is the memory?

In 2012, Xu Liu, Steve Ramirez, Susumu Tonegawa and colleagues tagged the small set of hippocampal cells that were active while a mouse learned to fear a place. Later, in a completely different box, they switched those cells on with light. The mice froze, as if remembering. A sparse set of cells was enough to bring the memory back: a physical trace of an experience, called an engram.

How long does it take to set?

Consolidation happens on several timescales at once.

Over yearsFrom hippocampus to cortex

Over longer spans, memories reorganize. The hippocampus helps bind an experience together, and gradually the cortex takes on more of the storage. That is why damage to the hippocampus tends to spare old memories and hit recent ones hardest. In people, this loss can reach back years; in rats and mice, days to a month or two. And as the last topic showed, a strong schema can make the hand-off happen much faster.

Two cautionsMice, fear, and live debates

The engram work is in mice, mostly with fear memories, a long way from a lecture hall. And the protein story has exceptions and live debates; for example, some drugs used to block protein synthesis also suppress nerve activity. What is solid is the shape: new memories start fragile and stabilize with time.

Treat new learning as fragile

Avoid piling a second demanding topic straight on top of the first. Give a short pause, a summary or a low-stakes question before moving on. Come back within a day or two, while the trace is still settling; the spacing and sleep page shows why. And connect new material to what students already know, so it settles into an existing frame.
