Learning to See Faces and words

On the underside of the brain, the visual system learns the things we see most. Faces get a dedicated patch. So, once we learn to read, do words.

In 2010, Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues scanned the brains of 63 adults while they looked at written sentences, faces, houses, tools and checkerboards. Thirty-one had learned to read as children, 22 had learned only as adults, and 10 had never learned to read.

In readers, a small patch of the left fusiform gyrus, on the underside of the brain toward the back, responded strongly to writing. The better people read, the stronger the response. Learning to read had changed what that part of the visual brain was for, even in people who learned as adults. It came at a small cost: in the same spot, the response to faces was slightly weaker in readers.

Neighbours on the undersideA face area and a word area

In 1997, Nancy Kanwisher, Josh McDermott and Marvin Chun found a patch of the fusiform gyrus that responded much more to faces than to houses and other objects. They found it in 12 of the 15 people they scanned and named it the fusiform face area. It is usually strongest on the right side of the brain.

The visual word form area sits close by on the left. In readers of every writing system studied, it turns up in roughly the same place. Because writing is too recent for the brain to have evolved a reading area, Dehaene calls this “neuronal recycling”: learning to read takes over part of a visual system that evolved for recognising objects, and retunes it for letters.

In 2018, Ghislaine Dehaene-Lambertz and colleagues scanned ten six-year-olds six or seven times through their first year of school. As children learned how letters map to sounds and their reading speed jumped in the first months, a word-selective patch appeared at the word area’s usual location. Words moved into a patch that had only weakly preferred anything, close to a face patch, and face responses grew stronger on the right side instead.

Words or not?

The flash gets shorter when you are right and longer when you miss, so it stays challenging. Guess if you’re not sure.

This is the word superiority effect, first reported by James Cattell in 1886 and pinned down by Gerald Reicher in 1969. Both letter choices fit equally well into each word, so knowing the word doesn’t let you simply guess. A fluent reader’s visual system takes in a familiar letter group as a unit, and a single letter is easier to catch inside it than inside a random string.

FacesExperts for upright faces

Faces are the other visual category most people see constantly. We are so practised with upright faces that we take them in as a whole. Turn a face upside down and that expertise largely disappears. In 1980, Peter Thompson flipped the eyes and mouth inside a photo of a face. Upside down, it looked almost normal. Upright, it looked grotesque. It became known as the Thatcher illusion, after the face he used.

Which face has been altered?

Some people have lifelong difficulty recognising faces, a condition called developmental prosopagnosia, or face blindness. It is usually quoted as affecting about 2 to 2.5% of people, though a 2023 study showed the figure depends heavily on where the cut-off is drawn. People with face blindness often learn to rely on other clues, such as a voice or a hairstyle.

Expertise you didn’t notice learning

Reading at a glance and recognising faces feel effortless because they rest on years of practice that shaped the visual brain itself. Beginning readers don’t have that yet. Sounding words out slowly is not a sign that something is wrong; it is the stage that builds the expertise.

In popular cultureSeeing faces, reading words

A face on another planet, an email that said letter order doesn’t matter, and a condition that keeps turning up in the news.

Why it matters for learningExpert seeing is learned

  1. Teach the codeThe word area grows as children learn how letters map to sounds. Make that link explicit.
  2. Lots of readingThousands of encounters with words train the visual brain to see letter groups at a glance.
  3. Patience with beginnersSlow, effortful reading is how the expertise gets built, not a sign it never will.
