Neuroplasticity Your brain is not finished

Every time you learn something, your brain physically changes. Connections strengthen, new branches grow, busy pathways speed up and unused ones fade. That is neuroplasticity, the foundation for everything else in this series.

In the late 1960s, in Per Andersen’s laboratory in Oslo, Terje Lømo and Tim Bliss sent short bursts of electrical pulses along a bundle of nerve fibres into the hippocampus of rabbits. Afterwards, a single pulse along the same fibres produced a bigger response in the cells on the other side. The connection had become stronger.

In their 1973 paper, the boost appeared in 15 of 18 rabbits and lasted from half an hour to 10 hours. It was the first direct evidence of what learning theorists had long suspected: the connections between neurons change with use. Today it is called long-term potentiation, and it is one of the main ways the brain stores what it learns.

At the synapseUse it, and it grows stronger

Learning happens at synapses, the tiny gaps where one neuron passes a signal to the next. When a connection is used again and again, the receiving cell responds more strongly to the same signal. That is long-term potentiation. Connections that are rarely used can weaken instead.

In 1949, the psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that when one cell repeatedly helps to fire another, the link between them grows stronger. The neuroscientist Carla Shatz later summed up the idea in a phrase that stuck: cells that fire together, wire together.

With longer practice, the changes go beyond stronger signals. Neurons grow new branches and new contacts. Well-used pathways gain more myelin, the fatty insulation that speeds up signals. And connections that go unused are pruned away.

Can practice beat the mirror?

Start at the orange dot and go all the way around, in either direction, back to the dot. Slips outside the band are counted.

This is the task Brenda Milner gave to Henry Molaison, the patient who lost the ability to form new memories of events. Over three days he got almost perfect at it, even though each time he had no memory of having done it before. Skill learning runs on its own circuits.

Improvements within a few minutes come mostly from changes in how strongly existing connections pass signals. The kind of growth that shows up in brain scans takes weeks of practice.

In brain scansPractice you can see

In 2004, Bogdan Draganski, Arne May and colleagues scanned 24 adults, then taught 12 of them to juggle three balls. After three months, once they could juggle for at least a minute, the jugglers had more grey matter in an area on both sides of the brain that processes visual motion, and in a groove of the left parietal lobe. The scans were repeated three months after they stopped practising.

Learning London’s layout, about 25,000 streets, takes trainee taxi drivers three to four years. In 2011, Katherine Woollett and Eleanor Maguire scanned 79 trainees and 31 non-drivers at the start, when the groups showed no differences, and again three to four years later. Only 39 trainees qualified.

Some cautionWhat scans can and can’t say

These are small studies. Scans measure volume, not exactly what grew, whether new connections, more support cells or something else. Bigger is not always better: the qualified taxi drivers did worse than non-drivers on some tests of new visual information. And these changes took months to years, not minutes.

Practice is construction

The brain changes in response to what it does, not what it is shown. A single exposure leaves a weak trace. Repeated, spaced practice, with learners doing the thinking themselves, uses the same pathways again and again. And because unused connections fade, old material needs revisiting.

Why it matters for learningPlan for change that takes time

  1. Repeat, don’t just exposePlan for repeated practice, not a single explanation.
  2. Space it outCome back to the same ideas over days and weeks so the same pathways are used again.
  3. Make learners do the thinkingThe brain changes in response to what it does. Revisit old material, or it fades.
