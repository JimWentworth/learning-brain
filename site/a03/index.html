In 2023, Fabrizio Dell’Acqua, Ethan Mollick, Karim Lakhani and colleagues gave 758 consultants at a global firm realistic tasks, some with an AI model and some without. On tasks inside the AI’s abilities, those with AI finished 12% more tasks, a quarter faster, at more than 40% higher quality.

But one task was chosen to sit just outside what the AI could do. There, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to get the right answer. The tool sounded just as confident.

A jagged frontierThe line is invisible from the inside

The researchers called this a jagged frontier. Tasks that look equally hard can fall on either side, and the line is invisible from the inside.

LevellingNovices gain most, and see least

AI also levels skill. Consultants below average improved by 43%, those above by 17%. In a study of 5,179 customer-support agents, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond found that AI raised productivity by 14% overall and by 34% for novices, with little effect for experts.

For learning, that levelling cuts two ways. Novices gain the most performance, and they are also the least able to tell when the AI is wrong.

Centaurs and cyborgsTwo ways that worked

Successful users worked in two ways. Centaurs split the task, deciding what to hand off. Cyborgs worked in constant back-and-forth. Both kept judging the output.

A cautionPerformance, not learning

These are workplace studies of performance, not learning, using models from 2023. The frontier has moved since, and it is still jagged.

Teach students to find the edge

Map your own assignments against the frontier: where does AI help, and where does it mislead? Give students one task deliberately outside it, so they learn to spot the edge. And require a check step before any AI answer is accepted.
