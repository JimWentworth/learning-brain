The surveysKnowledge is not immunity

In 2012, Sanne Dekker and colleagues surveyed 242 teachers in England and the Netherlands. On average, they believed 49% of the myths. Teachers with more general brain knowledge believed more myths, not fewer.

A larger American study in 2017, by Kelly Macdonald and colleagues, compared groups. The general public endorsed 68% of the myths, educators 56%, and people with high exposure to neuroscience 46%. Training helped, but did not eliminate them.

Why they stickFour reasons

Most start from a real finding. They offer a simple story and an easy fix. They are sold in training and products. And brain language makes a claim feel scientific.

This series takes twelve popular ideas, one at a time: where each came from, what the evidence shows, the grain of truth if there is one, and what to do instead.

For teaching

When a teaching claim mentions the brain, ask what was actually tested, and on what outcome. Look for the original study, not the summary. And be extra careful with ideas that sell a product. Share this tip with students: when a claim mentions the brain, ask how they know.
