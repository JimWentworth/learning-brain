Spacing and sleep Time apart makes it last

The same practice, spread out over time, lasts longer than practice crammed together. Spacing feels worse while you do it. And sleep, between and after sessions, is part of how learning settles in.

Memory fades fast at first, then more slowly. Hermann Ebbinghaus mapped this forgetting curve in the 1880s, testing himself on lists of nonsense syllables. Each review lifts the curve back up, and afterwards it falls more slowly.

That is the spacing effect: the same amount of practice, spread out, lasts longer than practice crammed together.

The gapHow far apart?

Spacing is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. In 2006, Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues reviewed 839 tests of it, from 317 experiments in 184 articles. In a 2008 follow-up, they taught more than 1,350 people a set of facts, reviewed them after a gap of up to 3.5 months, then tested them up to a year later. The best gap grew with how long the memory had to last. Measured as a share of the time until the test, it was about 20 to 40% for a test a week away, and about 5 to 10% for a test a year away. For a test in a week, that means reviewing after one to three days; for a year, after about three to five weeks.

The feelingSpacing feels worse

Massed practice feels smooth: the answer is still fresh each time it comes back. Spaced practice feels harder, because you have partly forgotten. In 2009, Nate Kornell had students learn word pairs with flashcards. Spacing beat massing for 90% of them, yet after the first session, 72% believed massing had worked better.

SleepPart of the schedule

In 1994, Matthew Wilson and Bruce McNaughton recorded place cells in rats. Cells that fired together while a rat explored fired together again during the sleep that followed, as if the hippocampus were replaying the day.

In people, sleep between sessions seems to help. In 2016, Stéphanie Mazza and colleagues taught 40 adults 16 French–Swahili word pairs, then had them relearn the pairs 12 hours later, either after a night’s sleep or after a day awake. Those who had slept needed about 3 rounds to relearn all 16, against about 6 for those who hadn’t, and they remembered more a week and six months later. And in 2007, Seung-Schik Yoo, Matthew Walker and colleagues found that one night without sleep cut the hippocampus’s response during new learning. The sleep-deprived group remembered 19% fewer of the pictures they studied.

Some cautionStrongest for facts

The evidence for spacing is strongest for facts and vocabulary. For complex skills, results are more mixed. And many sleep studies are small, so treat them as consistent signals rather than precise numbers.

Bring it back later

Return to old material in later weeks, not only in the unit where it was taught. Make exams cumulative, so spacing pays off. Start practice early and come back to it. Set deadlines that don’t reward all-nighters, and tell students plainly that sleep is part of studying.
