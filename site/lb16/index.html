In 1961, preschool children watched an adult attack an inflatable Bobo doll: punching it, sitting on it, striking it with a mallet. Later, alone with the doll, many of them copied those exact, unusual actions. Nobody had rewarded them. They learned just by watching.

Albert Bandura argued that watching is not passive. The learner has to attend to the model, retain what they saw, be able to reproduce it, and have a reason to. If any one of these fails, the learning doesn’t show up.

The brainMirror neurons, and a caution

In the 1990s, Giacomo Rizzolatti’s group, recording single cells in the premotor cortex of macaque monkeys, found neurons that fired both when the monkey grasped an object and when it watched someone else do so. They called them mirror neurons. In the most detailed early study, by Vittorio Gallese and colleagues in 1996, they were 92 of the 532 cells recorded, about 17%.

Claims that mirror neurons explain empathy, language or human learning went far beyond the data. Most human evidence comes from brain imaging, not single cells, and whether these cells are needed to understand actions is contested. The behavioural finding stands on its own: people learn by watching.

The classroomWatching, discussing, teaching

For novices, studying worked examples beats solving the same problems unaided, as the working memory page explains. As skill grows, the advantage fades and practice should take over.

Watching expert help can work too. In 2008, Michelene Chi, Marguerite Roy and Robert Hausmann found that students who watched videos of physics tutoring in pairs, talking it through, learned to solve problems as well as the students being tutored. Peers matter as well. In 2009, Michelle Smith and colleagues had genetics students answer a clicker question, discuss it with neighbours, then answer a new question on the same concept alone. Scores improved, even in groups where no one had known the answer at first. And in 2014, John Nestojko and colleagues found that learners told they would teach a passage recalled more of it, and organized it better, than learners expecting a test. Nobody actually taught.

Let students see how experts think

Show worked examples early, then fade them as students gain skill. Think aloud: make your reasoning visible, including the dead ends. Use peer discussion before you reveal the answer. And give students reasons to explain to someone else.
