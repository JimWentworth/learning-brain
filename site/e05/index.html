Feedback is often called one of the most powerful tools in teaching. But is all feedback helpful? The answer is a clear no.

In 1996, Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi reviewed more than 130 studies of feedback. On average, feedback helped. But about a third of the effects were negative: performance got worse after feedback. Feedback was most likely to backfire when it drew attention to the person rather than the task.

The big pictureIt varies enormously

A 2020 meta-analysis by Benedikt Wisniewski, Klaus Zierer and John Hattie pooled 435 studies with more than 61,000 participants. The average effect was medium, about 0.5, but it varied enormously. Feedback that told students how to improve, and on what, worked best. Praise alone, rewards and punishments did little.

LevelsTask, process, person

Feedback on the task says what is right or wrong. Feedback on the process says how to approach it better. And feedback about the person, like “you’re so smart”, tends to do the least. This fits wise feedback from the Belonging series: high standards, belief that the student can meet them, and specific next steps.

For teaching

Give fewer comments, but make them specific and actionable: one thing to keep, one thing to change. Focus on the work and the strategy, not the person. And build in a chance to use the feedback, like a revision or the next assignment. Share this tip with students: look for the next step in feedback, not the score.
