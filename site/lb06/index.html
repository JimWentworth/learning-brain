Working memory limits Why less is more

You can hold only a few things in mind at once, and they fade within seconds. Experts get around the limit by packing more into each chunk. Teaching that respects the limit leaves room for the ideas that matter.

Read these ten letters once, then look away and say them back: F B I C I A N A S A. Hard? Now read them as FBI, CIA, NASA. Three things instead of ten, and suddenly easy.

That is working memory: the small mental space where you hold and work with ideas right now. It is tiny, it fades fast, and it decides how much new material a learner can take in at once.

The limitHow much fits

In 1956, the psychologist George Miller noted that people can repeat back about seven items, give or take two. He also saw that the unit that counts is not a letter or a digit but a chunk: a group that means something to you. In 2001, Nelson Cowan reviewed the evidence again and argued that once people can’t rehearse or group items, the true limit is nearer four chunks. Prefrontal and parietal regions, including the intraparietal sulcus, are among the areas that stay active while people hold information in mind.

The fadeGone in seconds

Working memory also fades. In 1959, Lloyd and Margaret Peterson asked students to remember three consonants, then count backwards by threes so they couldn’t rehearse. After 3 seconds, about 80% of the letter strings were recalled. After 18 seconds, fewer than 10% were.

ExpertiseChunks are where expertise lives

In the 1940s, the Dutch psychologist Adriaan de Groot showed chess players a real game position for a few seconds, then asked them to rebuild it. Masters put back almost every piece. In 1973, William Chase and Herbert Simon repeated the test with a 5-second look: a master could place 20 or more pieces, a beginner about four. Then came the control. On boards with the pieces placed at random, everyone, master or beginner, placed only about three or four.

The master’s working memory was no bigger. Years of play had stored thousands of familiar patterns in long-term memory, so a cluster of pieces became one chunk. Knowledge makes working memory go further, but only for material in your own field.

TeachingLoad you can’t avoid, and load you can

John Sweller’s cognitive load theory, first set out in 1988, builds on this limit. Some load comes from the material itself: a hard idea with many parts that interact. That load can only be broken into smaller steps. Other load comes from how the material is presented, and that part is under the teacher’s control.

One example is split attention. When a diagram’s labels sit in a separate key, learners have to hold a name in mind while they search for it, so effort goes into the layout, not the content. Paul Chandler and John Sweller found that putting the text into the diagram itself helped learning. Decorative pictures and on-screen text that is read aloud word for word add load too.

Some cautionTwo limits of the theory

First, load is hard to measure. Studies use self-ratings of effort, speed on a second task, or body signals, and these don’t always agree. Ton de Jong, among others, has argued that this makes the theory hard to test. Second, what helps a beginner can hinder an expert. Slava Kalyuga and colleagues called this the expertise reversal effect: worked examples and full guidance help novices, but for learners who already know the material, the extra guidance becomes redundant and can make learning worse.

Spend the space on what matters

Give new material in small steps, put labels on the diagram, and cut what doesn’t teach. Start beginners with worked examples, then fade the help as their chunks grow, and give more freedom to the learners who are ready for it.
