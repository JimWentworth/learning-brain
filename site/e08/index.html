The case for cumulative exams What gets tested again gets remembered longer.

Students love unit exams. Study the chapter, take the test, move on. Cumulative exams, which cover everything so far, are much less popular. But which one helps students remember?

Read the transcript

In 2013, Maya Khanna, Amy Badura Brack and Laura Finken compared psychology courses with cumulative and noncumulative finals. Students who had taken cumulative finals scored higher on later tests of course content, even up to 18 months after the course.

That same year, Natalie Kudrna Lawrence taught two sections of introductory psychology. One had cumulative exams all term. Students in that section did better on the final, on chapter quizzes, and in their overall course grade.

Who benefitsLower-scoring students

In Lawrence’s study, the benefit showed up mainly for lower-scoring students. Cumulative exams seemed to push them into habits that high scorers already had.

WhySpacing and retrieval

Cumulative exams require students to return to earlier material, which is spacing and retrieval practice, both from earlier series. Material is reviewed, not dropped after the unit test.

A cautionA small evidence base

These were a small number of studies, mostly in psychology courses, and students were not randomly assigned to sections. Results in other subjects may differ. Cumulative exams can also feel overwhelming: students need help with how to review a whole course, not just a chapter.

For teaching

Make the final cumulative, and include some earlier material on every exam. Tell students in advance, and teach them to review weekly. Provide practice questions from earlier units. Share this tip with students: review old units every week.
