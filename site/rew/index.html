The Reward System Learning from surprise

Deep in the brain, a small group of dopamine neurons reacts not to rewards themselves, but to how much better or worse they are than expected. That surprise signal is one of the brain’s main teachers.

In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz and his colleagues recorded the activity of single dopamine neurons in monkeys. When a squirt of fruit juice arrived out of the blue, the neurons fired in a burst. Then the monkeys learned that a light or a tone came just before the juice.

Once they had learned it, the burst moved. The neurons now fired at the light, and barely responded to the juice itself. And when the light came on but the juice didn’t, their firing dipped below its normal level at the exact moment the juice should have arrived. The neurons weren’t signalling reward. They were signalling surprise.

Where it isA small group with a long reach

The dopamine neurons Schultz recorded sit in the midbrain, in two neighbouring areas called the ventral tegmental area and the substantia nigra. Their fibres reach forward to the striatum, including a part called the nucleus accumbens, and to the frontal lobes. Together these areas are often called the reward system.

The idea of a reward system goes back to 1954, when James Olds and Peter Milner at McGill University found that rats would keep pressing a lever when each press delivered a brief pulse of electricity to a particular spot in their brains. Pathways linked to dopamine neurons later turned out to be among the best places for this kind of self-stimulation.

Prediction errorA teaching signal

Dopamine neurons respond to the gap between what happened and what was expected. A reward that is better than expected produces a burst. One that arrives exactly as predicted produces almost no change. A reward that is worse than expected, or missing, produces a dip. Researchers call this gap a reward prediction error.

In 1998, Jeffrey Hollerman and Schultz followed this signal while monkeys learned new tasks. Early on, when rewards were unpredictable, the neurons fired strongly to them. As the monkeys got better and the rewards became predictable, the response faded. The signal matched a learning rule that computer scientists had already been using to train machines: change your predictions in proportion to how wrong they were.

How surprised will you be?

Your surprise is the payout minus your prediction: positive when it pays more than you expected, negative when it pays less.

In this game, surprise fades for lights that always behave the same way, because your predictions catch up. That is learning, and it is exactly what happened to the dopamine response in Schultz’s monkeys. A light that pays half the time is different: no prediction can stop it surprising you. In 2003, Christopher Fiorillo, Philippe Tobler and Schultz found that when a cue predicted reward with a 50:50 chance, dopamine neurons also showed a slow rise in activity while the monkey waited, strongest at exactly that 50:50 point.

Wanting and likingNot the pleasure chemical

Dopamine is often called the brain’s pleasure chemical, but the evidence points elsewhere. Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson removed up to 99% of the dopamine in parts of rats’ brains. The rats still made the normal facial reactions to sweet sugar that show enjoyment, even in human babies. What they lost was the motivation to go and get food. Dopamine seems to be needed for wanting rewards, and for learning about them, more than for liking them.

CuriosityWanting to know

Curiosity, the wish to know something, uses the same circuits. In 2014, Matthias Gruber, Bernard Gelman and Charan Ranganath asked 19 people to rate their curiosity about the answers to more than 100 trivia questions. In the scanner, each question was followed by a wait before its answer appeared, and during the wait an unrelated face was shown.

The more curious people were, the more the midbrain and nucleus accumbens became active while they waited. People remembered more answers to questions they were curious about. They also remembered the unrelated faces better, as if curiosity had put the hippocampus, the brain’s memory hub, into a state ready to learn.

RewardsWhen rewards backfire

If surprise and wanting drive learning, should teachers hand out rewards? It depends. In 1973, Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett picked out preschoolers who already liked drawing with felt-tip markers. One group was promised a “Good Player” certificate for drawing. A second group got the same certificate as a surprise afterwards. A third got nothing.

About two weeks later, the markers came back out during free play. Children who had been promised the certificate spent less time drawing than the other two groups. The children who had been surprised by it drew as much as those who got nothing. The promise seems to have turned drawing from something they enjoyed into something they did for a prize.

That doesn’t mean rewards always backfire. The effect was for an activity children already enjoyed, with a reward promised in advance. For tasks with little interest to begin with, some studies have found rewards can help.

Predict first

A prediction error needs a prediction. Asking learners to guess the answer, or predict the result of an experiment, before they find out sets up exactly the kind of surprise that drives learning, even when the guess is wrong.
