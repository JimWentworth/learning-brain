How words are learned

Nobody learns a word in one go. Each encounter adds a little: its form, its meaning, how it is used. Context helps, repetition helps, and deliberately recalling a word helps most of all.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Students read the sentences on their own devices without writing anything down.
  2. Run the meaning test. Ask: were the words you met three times easier?
  3. Now ask students to use two of the words in a sentence of their own, without looking. Which could they do?
  4. Connect to your subject: which key terms do students meet only once or twice?

Ask the room

  • Which words could you guess from one sentence? Which could you use yourself?
  • What did the second and third sentence add?
  • How many times do students meet each key term in your course, and in how many different contexts?

Connect it to your course

Plan for repeated, varied encounters with key terms: in the reading, in your talk, in problems, and in low-stakes quizzes where students must recall the term themselves.

Good to know

Guessing a meaning from a rich sentence is easier than knowing a word well enough to use it. The test here only checks recognition.

Words are mostly learned gradually. Researchers who study second-language vocabulary distinguish incidental learning, picking words up while reading or listening for meaning, from intentional learning, setting out to learn them.

Incidental learning works, but slowly. A 2023 meta-analysis by Stuart Webb, Takumi Uchihara and Akifumi Yanagisawa found average gains of roughly 15% of target words from reading or listening, on both immediate and delayed tests.

RepetitionEach encounter adds a little

Uchihara, Webb and Yanagisawa pooled 26 studies with 1,918 learners and found a medium relationship between the number of encounters with a word and how well it was learned (r = .34). How much each encounter helped depended on the learner, the spacing of the encounters, visual support and how learning was tested.

ContextQuality, not just quantity

In an earlier study, Webb gave learners target words in contexts that varied in how much they revealed. The informativeness of the context mattered more for learning a word’s meaning, while the number of encounters mattered more for learning its form.

RetrievalRecall beats rereading

In a well-known study, Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger had students learn 40 Swahili–English word pairs. A week later, students who had been repeatedly tested on all the pairs recalled about 80% of them, while those who kept studying but were not repeatedly tested recalled about 35%. Topic 7 looks at this in more detail.

For learningEvery subject has a vocabulary

This applies to the technical terms of any course, not only to foreign languages. A term met once in a reading is unlikely to stick; a term met several times, in different contexts, and recalled in a quiz, is far more likely to.
