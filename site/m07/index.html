Inside deep sleepWaves, spindles and ripples

Deep, slow-wave sleep is where replay happens most. Large slow waves sweep across the cortex. Short bursts called spindles ride on them, and the thalamus helps generate those. In the hippocampus, brief ripples carry the replay. Their timing lines up, which is thought to help move new memories from the hippocampus toward the cortex.

Steering the replayA scent in the night

In 2007, Björn Rasch, Jan Born and colleagues had people learn the locations of card pairs while smelling roses. During deep sleep that night, the scent was released again. The next day they remembered 97% of the locations, against 86% without the cue. The same scent during dream (REM) sleep, or while awake, didn’t help. This is called targeted memory reactivation.

A 2020 review by Xiaoqing Hu, Ken Paller and colleagues combined 91 experiments with about 2,000 people. The effect was real but small, under a third of a standard deviation, and showed up only in non-REM sleep.

Before learningSleep loss closes the gate

Sleep matters before learning too. In 2007, Seung-Schik Yoo, Matthew Walker and colleagues kept volunteers awake for a night. While learning new pictures the next day, they showed less hippocampal activity, and two days later, after catching up on sleep, they recognized 19% fewer pictures than people who had slept.

When did sleep matter?

A cautionLabs, not classrooms

Most of this comes from labs, with simple materials, and cueing memories during sleep isn’t a classroom tool. The reliable message is simpler: sleep after learning helps it stick, and sleep loss before learning makes it harder to get in.

Sleep is part of studying

Avoid deadlines that reward all-nighters, like midnight submissions before a morning exam. Teach students that sleep is part of studying, not time taken from it. And review hard material the day after it’s introduced, once sleep has had its first pass. The spacing and sleep page has more.
