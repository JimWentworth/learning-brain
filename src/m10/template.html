Divers learned lists of words either on a beach or under water, then recalled them in the same place or the other. In Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley’s 1975 study, recall was best when the two settings matched: words learned under water came back better under water.

Encoding specificityThe cue that was stored with it

When people learned a word alongside one cue, that cue brought it back better than another word that seemed more obviously related, as Endel Tulving and Donald Thomson showed in 1973. Meaning works the same way: read “The man lifted the piano” and something heavy is a good cue for piano; read “The man tuned the piano” and it isn’t. John Barclay and colleagues showed this in 1974.

PlaceSmall but reliable

How big is the effect of place? Steven Smith and Edward Vela’s 2001 review of 93 effects found it small but reliable, about 0.28 of a standard deviation. It shrinks when other good cues are present, and when people picture the original setting before recalling.

Context matters most for recall. When Godden and Baddeley had divers recognize the words instead, in 1980, the setting made no difference. And varying the setting can help: in 1978, Smith, Arthur Glenberg and Robert Bjork found that people who studied a list in two different rooms recalled more in a new room than people who studied twice in the same room.

A cautionA weak lever

Changing the room is a weak lever, and some studies find no effect at all. What matters more is whether the right cues are present at test, inside the material itself.

Build cues that travel

Practise retrieval in more than one setting and format, so knowledge doesn’t depend on one room or one slide. When students are stuck, have them picture where they learned it. And build meaningful cues into the material, like questions and categories, that will still be there at the exam.
