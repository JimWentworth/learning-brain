After brain surgery in 1953 to stop his seizures, a man known as H.M. could no longer form new memories of facts or events. The psychologist Brenda Milner gave him a hard task: trace a star while watching only its reflection in a mirror. Over three days, he got steadily better. Yet each day, he had no memory of ever doing it before.

H.M. showed that memory is not one system. He could learn a skill without being able to remember learning it.

Two systemsKnowing that, knowing how

Declarative memory holds facts and events: things you can put into words. It depends on the hippocampus, which is what H.M. had lost. Procedural memory holds skills and habits. It runs without awareness and leans on the basal ganglia, including the striatum, and the cerebellum.

In 1996, Barbara Knowlton, Jennifer Mangels and Larry Squire pulled the two apart. People learned to predict the weather from patterns of cards, where each card was linked to rain or sun only some of the time. Amnesic patients learned the task as well as anyone, yet could hardly answer questions about the training session. Patients with Parkinson’s disease, which affects the basal ganglia, showed the reverse: they remembered the session but failed to learn the task.

PracticeFrom slow to automatic

With practice, a skill shifts from slow and effortful to fast and automatic. That frees working memory for the next layer. A beginning reader sounds out each letter; a fluent reader stops noticing letters and starts thinking about the argument. Once reading is automatic, though, it is hard to switch off, as the next task shows.

The costAutomatic hides the steps

In 2002, Sian Beilock and colleagues asked golfers to putt while either attending to each step of the stroke or doing a distracting second task. Experienced golfers putted better when distracted and worse when attending to their stroke. Novices showed the opposite pattern.

Experts also lose sight of what was once hard. Mitchell Nathan and Kenneth Koedinger asked high-school maths teachers to rank algebra problems by difficulty. Most expected bare equations to be easiest. For students, equations were the hardest: fewer than 30% solved them, while more than half solved matching word problems. In 2003, Nathan and Anthony Petrosino found that future teachers with more advanced maths training were more likely to make this mistake. They called it the expert blind spot.

Some cautionFluency is not understanding

A student can run a procedure automatically and still not know why it works, or when to use it. Speed is worth building, but only for the right pieces, and only tied to meaning.

Choose what to make automatic

Decide which pieces students should make automatic, such as notation, core facts or routine steps, and drill those deliberately, spaced over time. Keep the reasoning in view, so fast steps stay tied to meaning. And when you explain, slow down and unpack the steps you no longer notice yourself.

Why it matters for learningPractice moves a skill out of the spotlight

  1. Choose what to automateNotation, core facts, routine steps.
  2. Drill it, spacedShort, deliberate practice across days and weeks.
  3. Unpack your own stepsThe steps you no longer notice are the ones students need to see.
