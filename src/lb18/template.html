A doctor must destroy a tumour with rays. A ray strong enough to kill the tumour will also destroy the healthy tissue around it. How?

In 1980, Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak gave people this classic puzzle, first posed by Karl Duncker, after they had read a story with the same solution. Most didn’t use the story. Told that it might help, about three in four solved the problem. That is transfer: using what you learned in a new situation. The knowledge was there. What failed was noticing that it applied.

Near and farWhy brain training disappoints

Students meet the same idea in new clothing and don’t recognise it. Near transfer, to similar situations, is common. Far transfer, to very different ones, is rare and hard won. That is also why brain training disappoints. In 2010, Adrian Owen and colleagues ran a six-week online study with 11,430 adults. People improved on every task they practised, but showed no gains on untrained tasks, even closely related ones. Practice makes you better at what you practise.

What helpsCompare, then name the principle

What helps is comparison. When people read two analogous stories and described what they had in common, they were much more likely to solve a new problem: they had built a general pattern, not just a memory of one story. In 2013, Louis Alfieri, Timothy Nokes-Malach and Christian Schunn pooled 57 experiments. Comparing cases reliably improved learning, especially when learners looked for similarities and were then given the principle.

Same structure, different surfaces

Teach with two or more examples that differ on the surface but share the deep structure. Ask students what the examples have in common, and name the principle after they compare. Mix problem types, so students must recognise which idea applies. And tell them plainly: this idea will show up again, in places that look different.
