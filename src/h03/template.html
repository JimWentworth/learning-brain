Read the transcript

Some people wake up sharp. Others hit their stride late at night. Researchers call this your chronotype: lark, owl, or somewhere in between. Among university students, owls are common, and evening types usually outnumber morning types.

At the National University of Singapore, Sing Chen Yeo, Joshua Gooley and colleagues used Wi-Fi logs to estimate lecture attendance for more than 23,000 students. Attendance at 8 a.m. classes was about 10 percentage points lower than at later times.

Early classesLess sleep, lower grades

Watch data from 181 students showed about an hour less sleep before an early class, because they woke earlier than usual. And across nearly 34,000 students, the more days a week they had morning classes, the lower their grade point average tended to be.

OwlsTiming hits owls hardest

In Dutch high schools, Vincent van der Vinne, Giulia Zerbini and colleagues found that late chronotypes did worse on early-morning exams, but the gap shrank later in the day. Lab studies call this the synchrony effect: demanding tasks tend to go best at your own peak time.

A cautionMostly correlations

These are mostly correlations. At one American university, morning types still had slightly better grades even after class times were taken into account. And chronotype can shift, even within a semester.

Try this

Notice when you feel sharpest, and put your hardest study there. If you can choose, avoid stacking early classes. If you can’t, keep your wake time steady so the early start hurts less. And save routine tasks for your low hours.
