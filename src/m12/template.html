At encodingGet it in, with meaning

Guard attention (topic 2, attention is the gate). Ask for meaning, not just form (topic 3, depth and meaning). Have students generate answers rather than copy them (topic 4, make it, don’t take it). And connect new material to what they already know (topic 5, building on what’s there).

Then give it timeLet it settle

Memories stabilize over hours and days (topic 6, from fragile to stable), and sleep does much of that work (topic 7, what sleep does). Don’t schedule against it.

Come backAfter some forgetting

In 2008, Nicholas Cepeda, Harold Pashler and colleagues taught more than 1,350 people a set of facts, reviewed them after a gap, and tested them up to a year later. The best gap grew with how long the memory had to last. As a share of the time until the test, it fell from about 20 to 40% for a test a week away to about 5 to 10% for a test a year away.

Choose how far away the test is. The calculator estimates a good gap between first learning and review, using the 2008 findings. Treat it as a rough guide: the study measured a few points, and this fills in between them.

Make retrieval the main eventRecall, vary, correct

Quiz often, ask for recall before showing answers, vary the cues and settings (topic 10, cues and context), and correct errors with feedback (topic 11, retrieval rewrites memory).

In 2013, John Dunlosky, Katherine Rawson and colleagues reviewed ten study techniques. They rated just two as high utility: practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice. Five, including rereading and highlighting, some of the techniques students use most, were rated low.

A cautionMeasure it in your course

Much of this evidence comes from labs and simple materials. Classroom studies mostly point the same way, but effects vary, so measure what changes in your own course.

For your next course

Pick one unit. Add a short weekly quiz, mix earlier topics into later quizzes, and swap one review session for retrieval. Then compare results with last year.
