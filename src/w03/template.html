Reading is learned, not natural

Speech comes naturally to children; reading does not. Writing is a recent invention, so the brain has to repurpose visual areas to read. Watching that happen, even in adults, says a lot about what fluency is.

Run it in class

  1. Students do the 12 words on their own devices and note how their time per word changed.
  2. Ask: how did it feel at the start? What were you paying attention to?
  3. Ask: in the last six words, without the key, what were you relying on?
  4. Connect to your subject: which notations, symbols or diagrams are a “new script” for your students?

Ask the room

  • What happened to your attention as decoding got faster?
  • What would it take to read this script as easily as English?
  • Which symbols in our field do beginners still have to decode letter by letter?

Connect it to your course

New notation (chemical formulas, mathematical symbols, musical scores, code) is read slowly and effortfully at first, leaving little attention for meaning. Give short, frequent practice reading it until it becomes fluent.

Good to know

Some letters are also flipped or turned versions of each other, which is part of what makes early reading hard.

Reading is too recent an invention for evolution to have built a dedicated reading area. Instead, learning to read changes how existing visual and language areas work.

Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues scanned the brains of 63 adults: 10 who were illiterate, 22 who had learned to read as adults, and 31 who had learned as children. Literacy increased the response to written words in a region of the left fusiform gyrus, often called the visual word form area, and strengthened the response to speech in an area of the temporal lobe.

RecyclingA new job for visual cortex

As literacy increased the response to writing in the left fusiform region, it produced a small competition with faces at the same location. Literacy also broadly enhanced visual responses in fusiform and occipital cortex, and gave spoken words a route to activate their spelling.

Never too lateAdult learners change too

Most of these changes appeared even in people who learned to read as adults. The authors emphasise that both childhood and adult education can profoundly refine how the cortex is organised.

FluencyWhy decoding speed matters

In the activity, the first words took effort: finding each symbol, holding letters in mind, then guessing the word. As the mapping became automatic, attention was freed for the word itself. That freed attention is what comprehension needs.

For learningEvery discipline has a script

Students meeting chemical structures, equations, musical notation or code for the first time are decoding a new script. Until reading it is fluent, little attention is left for meaning. Short, frequent practice reading the notation itself can pay off.
