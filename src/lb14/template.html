Two versions of a slide show tell nearly the same story. In one, a boy visits a hospital with his mother. In the other, he is badly hurt on the way and rushed into surgery. A week later, people remember the emotional version better, especially its middle, where the accident happens.

ArousalTagged as worth keeping

Emotional arousal releases stress hormones. The amygdala responds and signals the hippocampus to store the moment more strongly, as the amygdala page describes. Arousal tags an experience as worth keeping.

The costThinking goes offline

The same chemistry has a cost. Amy Arnsten’s research shows that even quite mild stress, if it feels uncontrollable, rapidly impairs the prefrontal cortex, the home of working memory and flexible thought, while strengthening fast emotional responses. Timing matters too. A 2017 meta-analysis by Grant Shields and colleagues found that stress shortly after learning can help memory, while stress just before or during recall impairs it.

Test anxietyWorry takes up working memory

That is why test anxiety hurts: worries take up the working memory the test needs. In 2011, Gerardo Ramirez and Sian Beilock asked ninth graders to write for 10 minutes about their worries just before their first high-school final exam. Among those who wrote, the most anxious students did about as well as their calmer classmates. In 2010, Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues told students preparing for the GRE graduate admissions test that feeling nervous could help them perform. They scored higher on the maths section, both in a practice test and on the real exam one to three months later.

Some cautionA sketch, not a law

The familiar inverted U, where a little arousal helps and too much hurts, is a useful sketch, not a precise law. Some emotional memory findings have not always replicated: a 1999 study using the same slide show found no effect of propranolol on memory. And chronic stress, lasting weeks or months, is a different problem from a tense exam.

Challenge without threat

Keep stakes clear and predictable. Spread grades across many small assessments rather than a few huge ones. Before big exams, give students a few minutes to write about their worries, and tell them that nerves are energy they can use.
