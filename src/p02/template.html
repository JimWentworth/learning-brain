Filling in: the blind spot

Each eye has a patch with no light receptors at all. You have never noticed it, because your brain fills the hole with a guess based on what surrounds it.

About 7 minutes to readTry it firstPredict the findings

Try it firstFind your blind spot

A cross, a dot and one closed eye. At the right distance, the dot disappears, and your brain paints over the hole.

Close your left eye. Keep your right eye on the cross.

Ready

Find your blind spot

Close your left eye and look at the cross with your right eye. Keep looking at the cross the whole time, and notice the target only out of the corner of your eye.

About 5–10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Students use their own screens; the right viewing distance depends on screen size. A laptop usually works at about 30 to 40 cm; a phone much closer.
  2. Everyone closes the left eye, looks at the cross with the right eye, and slowly moves closer to or farther from the screen until the dot vanishes.
  3. Move through the line and the orange patch. Ask what they saw in the gap each time.
  4. Debrief: the hole was always there. What did the brain put in it, and where did that guess come from?

Ask the room

  • Why have you never noticed this hole before?
  • What did your brain use to fill it in?
  • Where else might we fill a gap without noticing there was one?

Connect it to your course

An analogy, not a finding: when an explanation skips a step, students fill the gap with what seems to fit, often without noticing a step was missing. Ask students to explain a process back to you in full, and show every step in worked examples.

Good to know

A student with sight in only the left eye can do it the other way round: look at the dot, and the cross will disappear at the right distance. On a projector, the right distance is several metres, so it only works for some seats.

Where the optic nerve leaves the back of the eye there are no photoreceptors, so the retina cannot respond to light there. In the visual field, that patch sits about 15 degrees from the point you are looking at, toward the side of your head, and covers roughly 5 degrees across and 7 degrees high.

That is far bigger than the dot you just made disappear. Yet the world never shows a hole.

Why you don’t noticeTwo eyes, and a brain that fills in

The blind spots of your two eyes sit on opposite sides of where you are looking, so they don’t overlap: each eye covers the other’s hole. But the hole stays hidden even with one eye shut. That can only be explained by the brain compensating, filling the gap with what surrounds it: the colour of the background, the line running through.

The pointA seamless picture is partly made up

What you experience as a complete view is partly the brain’s reconstruction. Usually that reconstruction is a good one. It fails only when something important sits exactly in the gap.

For learningAn analogy worth using carefully

The blind spot is about vision, not reasoning. But it is a vivid way to make a point that also holds for understanding: people can fill gaps without noticing there was a gap. Students reading a dense explanation may join step 2 to step 4 and feel that it all made sense. The fix is to make gaps visible: ask them to explain back, step by step.
