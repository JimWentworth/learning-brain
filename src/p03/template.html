Looking without seeing: inattentional and change blindness

We feel that we see everything in front of us. Yet people regularly miss big, obvious things, a gorilla, a stranger, a change in a picture, when their attention is elsewhere.

Try it firstSpot the change

Two versions of the same picture alternate, with a blink in between. Something big changes. How long does it take you to see it?

 

Ready

Spot the change

The picture will flicker between two versions. One thing is different. Press “I see it” as soon as you spot it, then say what changed.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Project the student version. Tell the class that one object changes between the two pictures, and to raise a hand when they see it. Don’t point.
  2. Note roughly how long it takes for most hands to go up. Repeat with round 2.
  3. Before round 3, say out loud where to look. Compare how fast hands go up.
  4. Debrief: the change was big and repeated many times. What was missing until they found it?

Ask the room

  • Why was such a big change so hard to see?
  • What made round 3 easier?
  • When you change one part of a slide or diagram, how would your students know?

Connect it to your course

When you build up a diagram or change a slide, say what changed and point to it. Students can’t compare two versions they never attended to, so an unannounced change may simply not be seen.

Good to know

The picture flickers about once a second against a mid-grey screen. If anyone in the room is sensitive to flicker, describe the activity instead, or use the predictions section below.

In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris asked people to count basketball passes in a short video. Partway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked through the game. Across all the versions of the test, 46% of observers failed to notice the unexpected event.

This is inattentional blindness: failing to see something unexpected because attention is busy elsewhere. Its cousin is change blindness: failing to notice that something has changed.

The door studyA different person, mid-conversation

In 1998, Simons and Daniel Levin had an experimenter ask a pedestrian for directions. Two people carrying a door walked between them, and during that moment the experimenter was replaced by someone else. About half of the pedestrians did not notice they were now talking to a different person.

Even expertsThe gorilla in the scan

Trafton Drew, Melissa Võ and Jeremy Wolfe asked 24 radiologists to search lung scans for nodules. In the last case they inserted a gorilla 48 times the size of an average nodule. Twenty of the 24 did not report it, and eye tracking showed that most of those who missed it had looked directly at it. Experts are searching for something specific, and that focus works as blinders.

The flickerBig changes, many repeats

Ronald Rensink, Kevin O’Regan and James Clark put a brief blank between two versions of a scene, as in the activity above. People found even large, repeated changes very hard to identify. They found them much faster when given a verbal cue about where to look. Poor visibility was not the problem; attention was.

For learningLooking is not seeing

Students who are busy copying notes, or following one part of a demonstration, can miss what you most want them to notice. We don’t store a detailed copy of everything in view; we keep what we attended to. So say what to look for, and say when something has changed.
