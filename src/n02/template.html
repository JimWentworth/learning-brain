The claim is specific. Students learn better when teaching matches their preferred style. Researchers call this the meshing hypothesis.

To test it, you need a particular experiment. Sort students by style. Teach each group in both formats. Then give everyone the same test.

The evidenceA specific test, rarely passed

In 2008, Harold Pashler and colleagues reviewed the research. Very few studies used the crossover design. Those that did found virtually no evidence for meshing, and several contradicted it. Later reviews have reached the same conclusion.

Yet the idea is everywhere. In a 2014 survey, 76% of UK schoolteachers said they used learning styles. And there is a thriving industry of style tests, guidebooks and workshops.

The grain of truthPreferences are real

People really do have preferences, and differ in abilities. But the best format depends mostly on the content. A map is visual for everyone. A melody is auditory for everyone. And a format that feels easier is not always the one that teaches more, as Habits of the Learner showed.

For teaching

Choose the format that fits the content, and combine words with pictures where it helps. Use methods that work for nearly everyone, like retrieval practice and spacing. And drop style inventories from your course. Share this tip with students: pick study methods by what works, not by your style.
