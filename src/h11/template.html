Mindset, with realistic effects Abilities can grow; strategies make it happen

Growth mindset is on posters in many schools. The best evidence says its effects are real for some students in some settings, small on average, and depend on strategy, not effort alone.

Growth mindset is the belief that your abilities can develop through effort, good strategies and help from others. How much does teaching it actually change results?

The biggest test, led by David Yeager and published in 2019, involved more than 12,000 ninth graders across the United States. They did either a growth mindset program or a control activity: two short online sessions of about 25 minutes each. Among lower-achieving students, grades rose by about a tenth of a grade point, and the share with a D or F average fell by more than five percentage points.

ContextA seed that needs fertile soil

Both higher and lower achievers were also a little more likely to take advanced maths the next year. The effect on grades was larger in schools where classmates valued taking on hard work. Yeager compared the program to a seed that grows in fertile soil.

Meta-analysesA more modest story

In 2018, Victoria Sisk, Brooke Macnamara and colleagues found the average effect of mindset programs on achievement was tiny, about 0.08. In 2023, Macnamara and Alexander Burgoyne found that the highest-quality studies showed little or no benefit, and that authors with a financial stake were about two and a half times as likely to report positive results. Another 2023 meta-analysis, by Jeni Burnette and colleagues, found meaningful effects for students at academic risk. The debate continues.

A cautionOne ingredient, not a fix

Effects are small on average, and depend on context. A mindset is one ingredient, not a fix. And growth comes from effort plus better strategies, not effort alone.
