Dunning–Kruger, carefully Use it as a mirror, not an insult

“The less you know, the more confident you are” is a great story. The evidence is more interesting: random numbers can draw the famous graph, and everyone’s confidence is imperfect.

You have probably heard of the Dunning–Kruger effect: the idea that the less you know, the more confident you are. In 1999, Justin Kruger and David Dunning gave Cornell students tests of logic, grammar and humour. Those in the bottom quarter scored around the 12th percentile, but estimated they were around the 62nd.

But random numbers can draw the same picture. When Edward Nuhfer and colleagues fed computer-generated numbers into the classic graph, the famous pattern appeared anyway. Regression to the mean, plus most people rating themselves above average, is enough to produce it.

Better methodsNo special blind spot at the bottom

With better methods, Nuhfer’s team found that most of more than a thousand students and faculty judged their own performance fairly accurately. And in a study of 929 adults, by Gilles Gignac and Marcin Zajenkowski, self-rated and measured intelligence lined up along a straight line, with no special blind spot at the bottom.

So the safer lesson is not that some people are clueless. Everyone’s confidence is imperfect, especially in a skill you are just starting to learn. Dunning himself has stressed that the effect is about all of us, not other people.

The hopeful partTraining improved self-judgement

The original paper also found something hopeful. After brief training in logic, low scorers became far more accurate about their own performance.

A cautionStill debated

Researchers still debate how much of the effect is real, and some critics of the critiques argue the statistics cut both ways. But one fix works either way: compare your confidence with real results.

Try this

Before each quiz, predict your score, then compare. Mark the questions you felt sure about, and check them. Get feedback early, well before the exam. And never use Dunning–Kruger as an insult. Use it as a mirror.
