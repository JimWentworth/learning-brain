The gapAn error is the gap made visible

The brain learns from the gap between what it expected and what happened, as in the page on prediction. An error is that gap made visible. And confident errors are not the hardest to fix. In 2001, Brady Butterfield and Janet Metcalfe found that people were more likely to correct an answer they had been sure about, once they saw the right one. They called it the hypercorrection effect. A big surprise seems to grab attention for the correction.

Does a wrong guess help?

You’ll almost never guess right. That’s the point: the question is whether a wrong guess, followed by the answer, helps you remember.

Whole lessonsProductive failure

The same idea scales up. In productive failure, students wrestle with a problem before they are taught the method, and usually fall short. Then the teacher explains. In 2021, Tanmay Sinha and Manu Kapur pooled 53 studies with 166 comparisons. Problem solving first generally beat instruction first for conceptual understanding and transfer, and the advantage was larger when the method was used as designed. But not always: younger students, in primary school, did better with instruction first.

FeedbackErrors help only when feedback follows

Without feedback, an error can stick. Multiple-choice tests expose students to wrong options, and a wrong option chosen can later be recalled as if it were true. In 2008, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger showed that feedback after the test both increased correct answers later and reduced these intrusions.

Not all feedback helps, though. In 1996, Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi pooled 607 effects of feedback. On average, feedback improved performance, yet more than a third of the feedback interventions made performance worse. Feedback did least good when it drew attention away from the task and toward the person.

Some cautionErrors are not free

Errors help when they are followed quickly by clear correction. Left alone, they can be learned. And struggle before instruction suits some learners and topics better than others, so watch who is still lost when the explanation comes.

Make errors cheap, and correct them fast

Ask students to predict or attempt before you explain. Make errors ungraded, private and expected. Ask how confident they are, so surprising errors get attention. Follow every attempt with clear feedback, aimed at the work rather than the person, then let them try again.
