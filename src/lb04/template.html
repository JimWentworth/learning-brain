The power of prediction Learning from the gap

Your brain is always guessing what comes next. When the guess is wrong, the gap between expected and actual is the signal that drives learning. Once something is fully predicted, there is nothing left to learn.

Watch a ball roll toward the edge of a table. Before it falls, you have already pictured where it will land. The brain doesn’t just take in the world. It is constantly guessing what comes next.

One influential theory, predictive coding, says perception works this way. In a 1999 model of the visual cortex, Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard proposed that higher areas send predictions down to lower ones, and only the residual error, the part the prediction got wrong, travels back up. Whether the whole brain works like this is still debated. But in one place, the evidence for prediction errors is strong: learning from reward.

The evidenceCells that signal surprise

In monkeys, dopamine cells in the midbrain fire when juice arrives unexpectedly. After the monkey learns that a light comes first, the cells fire at the light instead and barely respond to the juice. And if the expected juice never comes, they go quiet at the moment it was due. They signal the difference between what was expected and what happened (see The reward system).

The ruleLearning in proportion to error

Decades before those recordings, in 1972, Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner proposed a model of classical conditioning built on the same idea. On each trial, how much an animal learns depends on how surprising the outcome was. A big error makes a big update. A small error makes a small one. When the outcome is fully predicted, the error is zero, and nothing more is learned.

The model explained a puzzle Leon Kamin had found: blocking. If an animal has already learned that a light means food, adding a tone alongside the light teaches it almost nothing about the tone. The food is no longer a surprise.

In the classroomWatching isn’t enough

Prediction errors aren’t just for animals learning about food. In 2004, Catherine Crouch, Adam Fagen, Paul Callan and Eric Mazur compared ways of running physics demonstrations in a large Harvard course. Students who simply watched a demonstration and heard it explained understood the underlying concept no better than students who never saw it at all. Students who first predicted the outcome showed significantly greater understanding.

A prediction gives the brain something to be wrong about. Without one, the result of a demonstration is just one more thing to see.

Some cautionStrong evidence, open theory

The evidence for reward prediction errors in dopamine cells is strong. The idea that the whole brain runs on prediction is still a theory, and an actively debated one. And the classroom gains from predicting were real, but modest.

Ask for a guess

Before you reveal a result, a demonstration, a worked problem or the answer to a question, have learners commit to a prediction, ideally in writing. A wrong guess isn’t wasted. It’s the signal that tells the brain to update.

Why it matters for learningThe brain learns from the gap

  1. Predict firstHave learners commit to a prediction before any demonstration or reveal.
  2. Write it downA written guess is harder to quietly revise after the fact.
  3. Value the missA wrong prediction is where the learning signal is biggest.
