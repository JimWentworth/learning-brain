In 2012, Jeri Little, Elizabeth Bjork, Robert Bjork and Genna Angello tested this. Students read passages, then took practice questions in multiple-choice or short-answer format, before a final test.

Both formats helped students remember the tested information. But multiple choice had an extra benefit. It also improved recall of related information that appeared only as the wrong answer options.

WhyThinking through every option

When the wrong options are plausible, students have to think through why each one is wrong. That means retrieving what they know about every option, not just the right one.

The key conditionCompetitive alternatives

If the wrong answers are obviously wrong, there is nothing to think about, and the benefit disappears. So the classic weak question, with one plausible answer and three silly ones, wastes the opportunity. So does “all of the above”, which can often be spotted without much thought.

For teaching

Write wrong options from real student misconceptions. Make every option plausible to someone who has not learned the material. After the quiz, discuss why each wrong answer is wrong. Share this tip with students: ask why each wrong answer is wrong.
