Gesture and learning

We talk with our hands, and listeners use what they see. Gestures that show shape, movement or location add information that words alone carry less well, and making gestures yourself may help even more than watching them.

Ask the room

  • What did gestures add that the words didn’t?
  • How did sitting on your hands change your explanation?
  • Which idea in this course has a shape or motion you could show with your hands?

Connect it to your course

Use your hands to show shapes, directions and changes over time, and point to what you are talking about. Encourage students to gesture when they explain, especially for spatial ideas.

Good to know

Gestures are not universal; some have different meanings in different cultures. Shape-and-motion gestures and pointing travel better than emblem gestures such as thumbs-up.

In 2019, Nicole Dargue, Naomi Sweller and Michael Jones pooled 83 independent samples that compared comprehension of speech with and without gestures. Across all of them, gesture had a moderate, beneficial effect on comprehension, whether the learner produced the gestures or observed them.

Which gestures were used, what information they carried, how comprehension was measured and the learner’s age all affected how much gesture helped.

Kinds of gesturePointing, picturing and beating time

Gestures that point direct attention to a place or object. Representational gestures picture a shape, size or movement, such as a curve rising steeply. Beat gestures keep rhythm with speech and add emphasis but no content. The first two carry information the words may not.

Doing beats watchingGesturing yourself

Studies in which learners produced gestures found significantly larger effects on comprehension, on average, than studies in which learners only observed gestures. Asking students to use their hands while explaining is a free addition to any discussion.

For learningHands as a second channel

For students learning in a second language, a gesture that pictures “increase”, “rotate” or “between” gives meaning a second route in. Gestures also tie to the face-to-face class: a reason to keep your hands, and yourself, visible on screen and in the room.
