Impostor feelings Feeling like an impostor is not evidence that you are one

Ever feel that everyone else belongs here, and you somehow slipped through? That one day you will be found out? That feeling is often called the impostor phenomenon.

The idea goes back to psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes in 1978. People with impostor feelings credit their success to luck or extra effort, not ability, and fear being exposed as a fraud.

In 2020, Dena Bravata and colleagues systematically reviewed 62 studies, with over 14,000 participants. Reported rates ranged from 9% to 82%. That huge range depended mostly on which questionnaire was used, and where the cutoff was set.

Men and womenboth commonly reported impostor feelings

What came with itDepression, anxiety, burnout

Impostor feelings were common in both men and women. They often came with depression and anxiety, and were linked to burnout and poorer job performance.

BelongingThe doubt grows in isolation

Impostor feelings and belonging doubts overlap. When students think they are the only one who feels this way, the doubt grows. Learning that others share it can help, as the belonging studies in topic 1 suggest.

The question is always: does it tell students that doubt is common, and that success comes from work they can see?

A cautionNot a diagnosis; no tested treatments

It is not a clinical diagnosis, and the measures vary widely. No study in the review tested a treatment. So be wary of quick fixes.

For teaching

Normalize doubt: say that struggle and uncertainty are common, even among experts. Share your own early struggles. Praise strategy and progress, and make the standards clear.
