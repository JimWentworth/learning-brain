In 1994, Larry Cahill, James McGaugh and their colleagues showed people 12 slides with a story. Some heard an everyday version: a mother and her son visit a hospital, where staff are practising a disaster drill. Others heard a version in which the middle was upsetting: the boy is badly hurt in a car accident and rushed into surgery.

A week later, in a surprise test, the people who heard the upsetting version remembered its middle especially well. People who had taken propranolol, a drug that blocks some effects of adrenaline, lost that advantage, while their memory for the everyday version was unaffected. Emotion was not just colouring the memory. Through the body’s stress hormones, it was helping to make it.

A boost for what mattersHow emotion strengthens memory

The amygdala is two almond-shaped clusters of cells, one deep in each temporal lobe, just in front of the hippocampus. Its name comes from the Greek word for almond.

When something stirs you, stress hormones such as adrenaline rise. Decades of research led by James McGaugh show that the amygdala responds to these signals and, in turn, helps the hippocampus and other regions strengthen the memory. The boost acts while a new memory is still settling in, a process called consolidation.

That timing leaves a fingerprint. In a 1998 study by Kevin LaBar and Elizabeth Phelps, people recalled emotionally arousing words better than neutral words, and the gap was bigger an hour later than straight away. People who had lost part of the amygdala in surgery did not show that growing advantage.

Learning what to fearThe feeling and the facts

The amygdala also learns what to fear. In 1995, Antoine Bechara, Antonio Damasio and colleagues at the University of Iowa showed people a color, followed by a loud noise, and measured sweating on the skin, a sign that the body expects something unpleasant.

One patient had damage to both amygdalae. She could tell the researchers exactly which color came before the noise, but her body never reacted to the color. Another patient had damage to the hippocampus. This patient’s body reacted to the color, but the patient could not say which color it was. A third patient, with damage to both regions, learned neither.

In 2011, Justin Feinstein and colleagues reported on patient S.M., a woman with damage to both amygdalae. They took her to an exotic pet store, a haunted house and scary films. She never showed fear and never reported feeling more than a little of it.

Narrowed and overconfidentWhat emotion does not do

Emotion does not record everything. In a 1987 study by Elizabeth Loftus and colleagues, people watched slides of a restaurant in which a man either handed the cashier a check or pointed a gun at the cashier. Their eyes kept going to the gun, and they were worse at picking the man out of a lineup afterwards.

Vivid memories can also mislead. On September 12, 2001, Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin asked 54 Duke students to write down how they heard about the attacks, and also an everyday event from the days before. When tested again weeks or months later, both kinds of memory had changed by the same amount. Only the students’ confidence in their memories of September 11 stayed high.

Fear and the workbench

Under stress, the same brain chemicals that weaken the prefrontal cortex strengthen the amygdala. A little arousal helps a memory stick. Fear narrows attention and makes it harder to think. That may be one reason anxious students can know the material and still struggle in a test.

In popular cultureMemories that won’t let go

A film about erasing a love affair, a film that taught millions to fear the water, and a question everyone thinks they can answer.

Why it matters for learningAim for interest, not fear

  1. Make it matterStories, real stakes and surprises engage the amygdala and help a lesson stick.
  2. Keep the stakes lowFear narrows attention and weakens the prefrontal workbench. Practise in low-pressure settings.
  3. Don’t trust the vivid feelingA memory that feels vivid isn’t always accurate. Check it by testing yourself.
