Two students each sleep about seven hours a night. One goes to bed at a different time every night. The other keeps a steady schedule. Who does better? The research says timing matters, not just hours.

At Harvard, Andrew Phillips and colleagues followed 61 undergraduates for 30 days with sleep diaries, and scored how regular each person’s sleep was from one day to the next. More regular sleepers tended to earn higher grades.

The body clockA 9 a.m. class at 6 a.m.

The surprise was the body clock. In the most irregular sleepers, the sleep hormone melatonin was released about two and a half hours later. So a 9 a.m. class could feel more like 6 a.m. And their average sleep length was not significantly different.

The likely driver was light. Regular sleepers got brighter light during the day and less light at night, which keeps the body clock anchored.

MITThe week, not the night before

At MIT, Kana Okano and colleagues gave activity trackers to 88 chemistry students for a semester. Better quality, longer and more consistent sleep all went with better grades. Together, sleep measures accounted for nearly a quarter of the variation in scores.

And the night before a test? It showed no relation to that test’s score. Sleep across the week and the month before is what tracked with grades. Going to bed after about 2 a.m. also went with lower scores, even with the same hours.

A cautionCorrelations at two universities

Both studies are correlations, each at a single university. Students who sleep regularly may differ in other ways too. But regular timing is cheap, safe, and in your control.

Try this

Pick a wake time you can keep seven days a week, weekends included. Get daylight soon after you wake. Dim screens and lights in the last hour before bed. And protect your sleep across the whole week before an exam, not just the night before.
