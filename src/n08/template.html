This is Brain Gym, a commercial program from the 1980s. Its short movements, like crawling, tracing shapes in the air, and yawning, are said to improve reading, focus, and coordination between the two halves of the brain.

Its explanations draw on older ideas: that repeating early movements “repatterns” the brain, that one hemisphere should dominate, and that training perception improves academic skills. Each of those ideas was tested decades ago and not supported.

The reviewNot supported by research

In 2007, Keith Hyatt reviewed the research on Brain Gym itself. He found only a few peer-reviewed studies, most with serious design problems, and concluded the program was not supported by research.

A larger pattern“Brain-based” labels

Brain Gym is one example of a larger pattern: products labelled brain-based or brain-friendly, sold with neuroscience language but little evidence that they improve learning.

The grain of truthMoving is good

Short bouts of exercise can give a small, brief lift in attention and mood, as Habits of the Learner showed. But that comes from the movement, not from repatterning the brain.

For teaching

Keep movement breaks if your students like them, and drop the brain claims. Before adopting any brain-based product, ask for controlled studies, published by people who do not sell it. Share this tip with students: take movement breaks, but skip the brain claims.
