First in the family Don’t hide the rules of college. Teach them.

First-generation college students arrive just as capable as their peers. But many have never been told the unwritten rules: what office hours are for, that asking for help is normal, how to talk to a professor.

At a selective private university, Nicole Stephens, MarYam Hamedani and Mesmin Destin ran a one-hour panel for incoming students. In both versions, senior students shared stories about adjusting to college. In the “difference-education” version, they also explained how their social-class backgrounds shaped those stories. One panelist said that because their parents had not gone to college, the family could not always give the advice they needed.

In the standard version, first-generation students ended the year with grades about 0.3 points lower than their peers. The difference-education panel closed that gap by 63%.

Why it workedAsking for help felt normal

First-generation students who heard it sought out more campus resources, and that help seeking accounted for their higher grades. Hearing how people like them had struggled, and coped, made asking for help feel normal.

Continuing-generation students benefited too. Students in the difference-education panel reported better well-being, engagement and social fit, and a deeper appreciation of different backgrounds.
