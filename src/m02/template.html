Watch a video of people passing a basketball, and count the passes. Partway through, a person in a gorilla suit walks through the middle of the scene. In Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris’s 1999 study, about half of viewers never saw it. Their eyes were on the screen; their attention was on the ball.

Attention is the gate to memory. The brain can’t take in everything, so it selects, and what isn’t selected is mostly gone before memory can work on it.

Where the gate sitsLearning needs attention far more than remembering

In 1996, Fergus Craik and colleagues had people learn word lists while doing a second task, reacting to lights. Splitting attention while learning sharply reduced what they remembered later. Splitting attention while recalling barely touched memory, though it slowed the second task. Learning needs attention far more than remembering does.

In the brain, a network linking the frontal lobe and the intraparietal sulcus, in the parietal lobe, steers attention on purpose: toward the ball, away from the gorilla. The attention page shows how what you select wins out over what you don’t.

In classDivided attention, all day long

Classrooms divide attention constantly. In a simulated lecture, Faria Sana, Tina Weston and Nicholas Cepeda found in 2013 that students who multitasked on laptops scored 11% lower on a test. Students who merely had a view of a classmate’s multitasking screen scored 17% lower, in a separate experiment.

From the insideMinds wander, sometimes on purpose

Attention also drifts from the inside. In recorded lectures, mind wandering tends to rise as the lecture goes on, and memory for later material falls. In live classes the picture is less clear. Across a semester of real lectures, Jeffrey Wammes and colleagues found in 2016 that students were mind wandering about a third of the time, with no steady rise within a lecture, and that more than half of it was by choice.

Guard the gate

Make the first exposure count: one demanding thing at a time. Set a device policy, and explain why, including the cost to neighbours. Tell students what to look for before you show it. Break long explanations with questions that need a response. Attention lets material in; what students do next, the subject of the next topic, decides what stays.
