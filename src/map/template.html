A Map of the Learning Brain

There is no single memory center. Every lesson you learn is spread across a team of brain regions, each doing its own part.

Ask where a memory is kept and most people picture a filing cabinet somewhere in the head: one drawer for names, another for skills, a third for the route home. It is a natural picture, and it is wrong in an interesting way.

There is no single memory center. A memory of your first day at a new school is not stored in one spot. Its sights sit in one part of the brain, its sounds in another, the feeling of nerves in a third, and what it all meant in yet another. When you remember the day, those pieces are pulled back together.

To see how learning works, it helps to know the team. This collection visits each member in turn. Start here, with the map.

The outer layerFour lobes, each with a job

The wrinkled outer layer of the brain is the cortex. Each side of it divides into four lobes. The frontal lobe, behind your forehead, plans and holds ideas in mind while you work with them. The parietal lobe, across the top, tracks space, attention and number. The temporal lobe, by your ear, handles sound, language and the meaning of things. The occipital lobe, at the back, is where seeing begins.

Underneath sit older structures. The hippocampus helps form new memories of facts and events. Beside it, the amygdala marks what feels important. The basal ganglia, including the striatum, build habits. The cerebellum, tucked below the back of the brain, fine-tunes movement and timing.

The wiring
86
billion neurons

That is the average count in adult brains studied by Frederico Azevedo and colleagues in 2009, lower than the often-quoted 100 billion.

Where they are

  • Cerebellum: about 69 billion (80%)
  • Cerebral cortex: about 16 billion (19%)
  • Everything else: about 1 billion

The cortex makes up 82% of the brain’s weight but holds only 19% of its neurons. The small cerebellum, at the back, is packed far more densely.

How they connect

100 trillion+

Each neuron forms thousands of connections, called synapses, adding up to an estimated 100 trillion or more. A single Purkinje cell in the cerebellum can have about 100,000. Learning changes the strength of these connections.

How far the wiring runs

~150,000 km

The insulated fibres linking distant regions add up to about 149,000 km in young women and 176,000 km in young men: roughly four times around the Earth’s equator.

How long to count them

2,700 years

Counting one neuron per second, day and night, it would take about 2,700 years to reach 86 billion.

In popular cultureA century of memory on screen

Film has been drawn to memory since the silent era. A 2004 review found amnesia in 60 films made between 1915 and 2004, and most of them got it wrong. Over time the portrayals have moved from the bump on the head toward the real science. Here are five turning points, with links to where this collection covers the real science.

A memory is not kept in a place. It is rebuilt from many.

What damage revealsDifferent kinds of learning

Some of the clearest evidence for this map comes from people whose brains were damaged in one place. What they lost, and what they kept, shows which regions each kind of learning depends on.

The patient known as H.M.

In 1953 a surgeon removed tissue from deep inside both temporal lobes of a 27-year-old man, Henry Molaison, to stop severe epileptic seizures. The seizures eased. But from then on he could not form new memories of facts or events, and he lived with that loss for 55 years.

Yet when the psychologist Brenda Milner asked him to trace a star while watching his hand only in a mirror, he improved within and across three days of practice, even though each session felt new to him. Skill learning did not need the hippocampus.

A 1996 study by Barbara Knowlton, Jennifer Mangels and Larry Squire made the split sharper. People with amnesia and people with Parkinson's disease, which damages the basal ganglia, learned a card-based task that rewards slow, habit-like learning.

Habits and facts run on different systems, and either can work while the other fails.

Why it matters for learningEngage many regions at once

The strongest learning recruits many regions: seeing a diagram, hearing an explanation, doing the steps, caring about the outcome and explaining it in your own words. Each adds another route back to the memory. Every lesson is whole-brain work.
