Explaining to learn

Students who explain each step of an example to themselves, why it works and not just what it does, learn more from it. That habit can be prompted, and putting it into words is the point.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Show the worked example and run the three steps with the class voting on the best explanation.
  2. Before revealing the transfer problem’s answer, have pairs explain their reasoning to each other.
  3. Point out the difference between paraphrasing a step and explaining why it works.
  4. Give a worked example from your own course with a prompt after each step: “Why this step?”

Ask the room

  • What makes an explanation “why” rather than “what”?
  • Which explanation would have helped you on the new problem?
  • Where in this course could you add a “why this step?” prompt?

Connect it to your course

Add short self-explanation prompts to worked examples, readings and solutions: “Why does this step work?” “What principle is used here?” Pair students to explain steps to each other, then check their explanations.

Good to know

Students learning in a second language may find explaining aloud harder; let them write first, or explain in pairs before sharing.

In a classic 1989 study, Michelene Chi and colleagues recorded students thinking aloud as they studied worked examples in a physics textbook. The students who learned more, the “good” students, generated many explanations, linking each step to the principles in the text and noticing when they didn’t understand. The weaker students mostly read and paraphrased.

A follow-up in 1994 found that prompting students to explain to themselves improved their understanding.

The evidenceA reliable effect

Kiran Bisra, Qing Liu, John Nesbit, Farimah Salimi and Philip Winne analysed 64 research reports with nearly 6,000 participants that compared prompted self-explanation with other conditions. The average effect was g = 0.55, a medium effect that held across most domains and settings. Most of the studies involved undergraduate students.

Why it worksInferences, not paraphrases

Self-explanation means generating inferences about causal connections and conceptual relationships: why a step works, what principle it uses, how it links to what you already know. Paraphrasing a step repeats it; explaining it builds the connection that a new problem will need.

TalkExplaining to others

Explaining to a partner adds an audience who can ask “why?” and expose gaps. It also gives students learning in a second language practice using the discipline’s language in a low-stakes setting, where the focus is on ideas rather than polish.

For learningPrompt the why

Students rarely self-explain unprompted, especially when a worked example looks easy. A simple prompt after each step, “why does this work?”, turns reading into reasoning.
