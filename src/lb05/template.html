You’ve been stuck on a problem for an hour. You give up, go for a walk, and halfway down the block, the answer shows up. Why does that happen?

One popular way to describe it uses two modes, from the engineering educator Barbara Oakley. Focused mode is tight and effortful: it follows familiar paths quickly, but only nearby. Diffuse mode is relaxed and wide: it makes long, loose jumps between distant ideas. This is a teaching metaphor, not a map of the brain. But it is loosely tied to something real.

The networksTask and rest

During demanding tasks, a set of attention regions in the frontal and parietal lobes switches on. At rest, a different set, the default mode network, mostly along the brain’s midline, becomes more active. In 2005, Michael Fox, Marcus Raichle and colleagues showed that even at rest, with no task at all, these two networks tend to rise and fall in opposition. The default network is busy when the mind wanders, remembers the past and imagines the future.

BreaksDoes stepping away help?

In 2009, Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod pooled decades of experiments on incubation, the idea that setting a problem aside helps solve it. Breaks did help, modestly. They worked best after a longer first attempt, and when the break was filled with an easy task rather than a demanding one. For word puzzles, an easy task even beat simple rest.

SleepSleeping on it

Sleep may help too. In 2004, Ullrich Wagner, Jan Born and colleagues had people practise a number task with a hidden shortcut they weren’t told about. After training, they spent 8 hours either asleep at night, awake at night, or awake during the day. Then they practised again. Of those who had slept, 59% found the shortcut, more than twice as many as in the groups that stayed awake. Sleep didn’t help people who hadn’t trained on the task first.

Some cautionA picture, not a mechanism

Some later sleep studies found smaller effects, or none. And creative thinking doesn’t simply flip between modes: in 2016, Roger Beaty and colleagues reviewed evidence that the default and control networks, which often work in opposition, tend to cooperate while people generate and evaluate creative ideas. Treat focused and diffuse as a useful picture, not a description of how the brain works.

Stuck is not failing

Give learners a hard problem early, let them work on it in earnest, then build in a gap, such as a break, a night or a different task, before coming back to it. And say plainly that being stuck is a normal stage, not a sign of failure.

Why it matters for learningLearning needs a rhythm

  1. Start hard problems earlySo there is time to step away and come back.
  2. Work in earnest firstBreaks help most after a real attempt.
  3. Make the break easyAn undemanding task, a walk, or a night’s sleep, not another hard task.
