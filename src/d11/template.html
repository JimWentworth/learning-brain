Lecture recordings A recording keeps the lecture. Only attending keeps the class.

Lecture recordings are now standard at many universities. Students love them. But do they change who shows up, and what students learn?

In a 2019 study, Martin Edwards and Michael Clinton compared two matched groups in the same course: 161 students before recordings were introduced, and 160 after.

After recordings arrived, attendance dropped substantially. Attendance predicted grades. And watching recordings did not make up for the lectures students missed. The authors concluded that, for this group, the net effect was generally negative.

A second studyA more nuanced picture

A 2019 study by Emily Nordmann and colleagues found a more nuanced picture. Higher-achieving students could use recordings to make up for missed lectures. Lower-achieving students benefited only when they used recordings on top of the lectures they attended. And in that study, recordings did not reduce attendance.

Why recordings matterReview, not replacement

Recordings still matter. They support students with disabilities, students who are ill or working, and anyone reviewing a hard section. The problem is not the recording. It is using it as a replacement for class, often in a late binge before an exam.

A binge is massed practice, and spacing works better. One long cram is the least effective way to use a recording.

A cautionTwo studies, different results

These are two studies in particular courses, with different results on attendance. Neither was a randomized trial. The safest reading is the one they share: recordings help most as a supplement, not a substitute.

For teaching

Keep recordings, and say plainly how to use them: to review and fill gaps, not to replace attending. Make class time worth attending, with activities a recording cannot capture. Suggest a weekly review rather than a binge. Share this tip with students: use recordings to review, not to replace class.
