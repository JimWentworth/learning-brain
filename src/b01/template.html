Read the transcript

Psychologists call this belonging uncertainty. When students aren’t sure they fit, everyday setbacks, like a low grade or a cold remark, can feel like proof. The worry is more common for students whose group is underrepresented, or negatively stereotyped, in that setting.

In a classic study, Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen tested a one-hour exercise with 92 first-year students at a selective university. Students read surveys and stories from older students: worries about belonging were common at first, and faded with time. Then they wrote an essay and gave a short speech, in their own words, for future students.

The messageNormal, shared, and it changes

Over the next three years, the exercise raised the grades of the African American students, and cut their achievement gap with white students by about half. They also reported better health, and fewer doctor visits. And in senior year, they showed no awareness that the exercise had made a difference.

The message never said college is easy. It said: struggle is normal, it is shared, and it changes. That changed how students read their own setbacks.

A cautionOne campus, a small sample

This was one campus and a small sample. Later and larger studies, in the next topic, found smaller effects that depended on the setting. A message cannot replace a course that actually welcomes students.

For teaching

Early in the term, tell students that most people find the start hard, and that it gets better. Share stories from former students, including students like them. Have students write advice to next year’s class. And respond to early setbacks as normal, not as a verdict.

Why it matters for learningBelonging isn’t a trait students have or lack. It is a story, and stories can change.
