The Transparency in Learning and Teaching project, started by Mary-Ann Winkelmes at the University of Chicago and expanded at the University of Illinois, asks instructors to make three things explicit for each assignment: its purpose, the task, and the criteria for success.

The studyJust two assignments

In a national study published in 2016, instructors at minority-serving institutions made just two assignments more transparent during a term. Students in the more transparent courses reported greater academic confidence, a stronger sense of belonging, and more awareness of the skills they were building. The benefits were larger for first-generation, low-income and underrepresented students.

WhyDecoding takes energy

Some students arrive knowing the unwritten rules of college assignments. Others spend their energy decoding the instructions instead of doing the work. Rubrics and examples are part of transparency: sharing what good work looks like, and why, helps students aim for it.

A cautionSelf-reported, not randomized

Many of the outcomes were self-reported, and the studies were not randomized trials. But the change is small, low cost, and consistent with the belonging research in the Belonging series.

For teaching

For your next assignment, write one sentence on its purpose, a short list of steps, and the criteria you will use. Share an annotated example. Discuss it in class before students begin. Share this tip with students: ask why this task matters, and what good looks like.
