Twenty-seven radiologists read 50 mammograms, each with a rating suggested by what they were told was an AI system. In 12 of the cases, the suggestion was deliberately wrong. Thomas Dratsch and colleagues reported the results in 2023.

When the AI was right, all three experience groups were about 80% accurate. When it was wrong, inexperienced readers fell to about 20%, moderately experienced readers to about 25%, and even very experienced readers, with more than 15 years on average, to about 45%.

Automation biasThe pull of the machine’s answer

This is automation bias: the pull to accept a machine’s answer over your own judgement. It shows up in two ways: acting on a wrong suggestion, and failing to notice what the system missed.

It is a shortcut. Checking takes effort, and a confident suggestion lowers the felt need to check. Expertise helps, but it does not make anyone immune.

SafeguardsTraining, feedback, and AI as a second check

An editorial alongside the study suggested safeguards: training; accountability, with regular feedback on performance; transparent and validated systems; and AI that runs in the background as a second check, rather than speaking first.

A cautionOne experiment, decades of evidence

This was one experiment, with a purported AI and a small number of readers. But automation bias itself has been studied for decades, from aviation to medicine.

Teach students to disagree

Have students commit to their own answer before they see the AI’s. Seed some AI suggestions with deliberate errors, and debrief who caught them. And give feedback on how often students overrode the AI when they should have.
