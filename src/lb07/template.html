Storytelling Why stories stick

Turn a list into a story and it becomes far easier to remember. Stories are also understood and recalled better than essays. But a story only helps if the key idea is part of the plot.

In 1969, Gordon Bower and Michael Clark gave people 12 lists of 10 unrelated nouns to learn. One group studied and rehearsed each list in the usual way. The other group wove each list into a little story. Right after each list, both groups recalled every word.

Then came a surprise test on all 12 lists. The rehearsal group recalled a median of about 13% of the words. The story group recalled 93%.

StructureA shape the brain already has

Why would a story help? A story has a built-in structure: someone wants something, something gets in the way, and one event causes the next. Each word in a story list is tied to the one before by cause and effect, so remembering one brings back the next. Rehearsed words have no such links, and lists learned the same way blur into each other.

You’ll learn two lists of 10 words, one minute each. For one list, you’ll repeat the words to yourself. For the other, you’ll make up a story that links them in order. You’ll recall each list straight away, and again at the end. About 5 minutes.

Beyond listsIt holds for text, too

In 2021, Raymond Mar and colleagues at York University pooled studies that compared stories with essays, or expository text, on the same material. Across more than 75 samples and more than 33,000 readers, stories were understood and remembered better.

The brainImaging hints at why

In 2011, Yulia Lerner, Uri Hasson and colleagues played people a real story in a brain scanner, either intact or cut up and shuffled at the level of words, sentences or paragraphs. Early hearing areas in the temporal lobe responded the same way each time, whatever the order, because they follow the sound. Areas higher up responded reliably only to longer stretches. At the top, regions in the frontal and parietal lobes responded reliably only when whole paragraphs came in their meaningful order. These areas seem to build meaning over minutes, not moments.

Some cautionThe wrong cargo

A story is not automatically good for learning. In 1998, Shannon Harp and Richard Mayer gave students a passage on how lightning forms. Some versions included vivid but irrelevant stories, such as a golfer struck by lightning. Across four experiments, students given those details recalled fewer of the main ideas and solved fewer problems. Highlighting the main ideas or stating the learning goals did not fix it. Moving the details to the end of the passage helped; putting them at the start made things worse.

There is a second risk: students can remember the story and forget the concept. The story helps only if the idea to be learned is what drives the plot.

Make the key idea the turning point

Frame a topic as a problem someone faced. Make the concept the thing that solves it, keep only the details that move that plot forward, and hold back the answer long enough for learners to want it.
