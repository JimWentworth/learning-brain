Expectation shapes what you see

The same shape can be a letter or a number. The same noisy sound can be a word you never heard. What you expect fills in what the senses leave open, for you and for every student in the room.

Try it firstLetter or number?

A few characters flash by. The last one is the same shape every time. What it looks like depends on what came before.

Ready

Letter or number?

A short series of characters will flash in the centre. Watch them all. Then say what the last one was.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Split the class into two halves. Have one half close their eyes while you show the other half round 1 (letters). Swap for round 2 (numbers).
  2. Each half writes down what the last character was, then compare. The two halves usually disagree.
  3. Show both side by side: the shape is identical.
  4. Finish with “THE CAT” and ask how the same shape became two different letters.

Ask the room

  • How can the same shape be two different things?
  • What did the earlier characters do to your brain?
  • In this course, where might students “see” what they expect rather than what is there?

Connect it to your course

Expectations are set by what comes first. Tell students what kind of thing they are about to see (“this graph shows a decline”, “the next term is a technical one”), and watch for students reading their misconceptions into new data.

Good to know

The original experiments flashed the shape very briefly. On a slow projector the effect may be weaker; the side-by-side reveal still works.

In a classic 1955 study, Jerome Bruner and Leigh Minturn briefly showed students a broken capital B, with the curved part set slightly apart from the upright stroke, so it could also be read as 13. Students who had just seen letters tended to report a B; those who had just seen numbers tended to report 13.

A 2020 study confirmed the effect: the same shape was judged 13 between 12 and 14, and B between A and C. Context still biased judgements even when people did not consciously see it, though the effect was smaller.

In hearingThe sound that wasn’t there

In 1970, Richard Warren played students a recording of the sentence “The state governors met with their respective legislatures convening in the capital city.” One sound, the first “s” in “legislatures”, had been cut out and replaced with a cough. In a group of 20, 19 did not notice anything was missing, and listeners could not say where in the sentence the cough had occurred. The brain restored the sound from what the word had to be.

WhyPrediction fills the gaps

Signals from the senses are often incomplete or ambiguous: a smudged letter, a word lost in noise. The brain resolves them with what is likely in context. Usually that makes perception faster and more robust. It also means we can perceive what we expect, not what is there.

For learningSet the right expectation

Students also perceive through their expectations. A student who expects a graph to show growth may read growth into it; a student who has never heard a technical term may hear a familiar word instead. Saying what is coming, and naming new terms before using them, gives the brain the right context to fill in from.
