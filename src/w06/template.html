The bilingual advantage, carefully

For years, headlines said that speaking two languages sharpens the mind’s control systems. Then researchers checked which studies got published. The story of the bilingual advantage is a lesson in how evidence can mislead.

You will simulate 40 small studies of a bilingual advantage, in a world where there is no advantage at all. Then you will decide which ones get published.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Project the student version. Run the 40 studies and point out that the average sits near zero.
  2. Ask the class which studies they think would get published. Then publish only the striking ones.
  3. Ask: if you read only the published studies, what would you conclude?
  4. Connect to how students evaluate evidence: one study, or many? Published only, or all?

Ask the room

  • Why did some studies show an advantage when the true effect was zero?
  • What does the published literature look like if only striking results get out?
  • How would you check for this when you read research?

Connect it to your course

Use this as a lesson in evaluating evidence, whatever the subject: single studies vary by chance, and what gets published can be a biased sample. Look for meta-analyses that check for publication bias, and for preregistered studies.

Good to know

The simulation is random, so each run differs. This is about how evidence can mislead, not a claim that bilingualism has no value: speaking two languages has obvious personal, social and professional benefits.

The idea was appealing: constantly managing two languages might train the brain’s executive functions, such as inhibiting distractions and switching between tasks. Many studies reported such an advantage. Others found none.

In 2018, Minna Lehtonen and colleagues pooled 891 effect sizes from 152 studies of adults. Before correcting for publication bias, they found a very small bilingual advantage for inhibition, shifting and working memory. After correcting for it, no evidence for a bilingual advantage remained.

Publication biasFollowing studies from talk to journal

Angela de Bruin, Barbara Treccani and Sergio Della Sala looked at 104 conference abstracts on bilingualism and executive control from 1999 to 2012, and checked which studies were later published. Studies fully supporting a bilingual advantage were the most likely to be published, followed by those with mixed results; studies challenging it were least likely. The difference was not explained by sample size, tests used or statistical power.

The detailsA small disadvantage, too

Lehtonen and colleagues also found a disadvantage for verbal fluency, possibly reflecting less exposure to each language for people who use two. And their analyses of possible explanations did not support the theory behind the advantage.

The lessonHow a literature can mislead

No one needs to cheat for this to happen. Small studies vary by chance; striking results are more exciting to write up and publish; and a reader who sees only the published studies sees a biased sample. The activity above shows how far that can shift the picture.

For learningValue bilingualism for the right reasons

Speaking more than one language opens communities, literatures and careers. Bilingual students don’t need a hidden brain-training benefit to justify supporting their languages. And this case is a good one for teaching students to read research critically.
