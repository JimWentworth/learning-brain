Teamwork How a memory moves through the brain

No single part of the brain holds a memory. The hippocampus ties an experience together, other areas mark what matters, and over nights of sleep and months of time the memory settles into the cortex, changing a little each time we recall it.

In 1953, the surgeon William Scoville removed most of the hippocampus, and nearby tissue, from both sides of the brain of a 27-year-old man named Henry Molaison. The aim was to control his severe epilepsy, and the seizures did become much less frequent. But Henry could no longer form lasting new memories of events. For the rest of his life, he met the people who studied him as if for the first time.

Yet much of his past survived. He knew who he was, and he could still talk about his childhood. Only the years just before the surgery were patchy. Whatever the hippocampus does, it could not be the place where all memories are kept for good.

Where it startsThe hippocampus ties it together

An experience arrives in pieces. Different parts of the cortex handle what you see, what you hear and what it means. The hippocampus, deep inside each temporal lobe, links those pieces into a single episode, so that one part, a smell or a song, can later bring back the rest.

Henry’s case showed that this linking is only one kind of memory. With his hippocampus gone, he still had normal intelligence and attention, and he could still learn skills.

The teamWho else is involved

The hippocampus doesn’t work alone. The amygdala marks emotional moments as important, which is one reason they stick (see The amygdala). The dopamine system flags surprise, reward and curiosity, and in curious states it works more closely with the hippocampus (see The reward system). The prefrontal cortex helps organise what we learn and later search for it. And the cortex itself is where the pieces live.

SleepReplaying the day

Much of the moving seems to happen while we sleep. In 1994, Matthew Wilson and Bruce McNaughton recorded groups of place cells in the hippocampus of three rats as they explored. Cells that fired together during exploring were more likely to fire together again in the deep, slow-wave sleep that followed than in the sleep before.

In people, the same kind of reactivation can be nudged. In 2007, Björn Rasch, Jan Born and colleagues had volunteers learn the locations of pairs of picture cards while smelling roses. When the scent was given again during slow-wave sleep, they remembered 97% of the locations the next day, compared with 86% without it. The scent did nothing when given during REM sleep or while awake.

Over timeFrom hippocampus to cortex

Over weeks to years, links between the pieces within the cortex grow stronger, and many memories come to depend less on the hippocampus. This gradual shift is called systems consolidation, and it fits Henry’s pattern: the older a memory was, the more likely it was to survive. Researchers still debate whether all memories make the move, especially vivid personal memories of events.

RebuiltEvery recall is a rewrite

Consolidated memories aren’t fixed for good. In 2000, Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe and Joseph LeDoux trained rats to fear a tone. Later, they played the tone again to bring the memory back, and then blocked protein-making in the amygdala. The memory was lost, but only if it had just been recalled. Recalling it had made it fragile again, so it had to be stored again, a process called reconsolidation.

Each act of remembering rebuilds the memory, which is why practising recall strengthens it. But it also means memories can drift. Remembering involves reconstruction, and sometimes we fill in what probably happened.

In 1995, Henry Roediger and Kathleen McDermott found that people recalled the missing word about 40% of the time, and later recognised it with high confidence. The brain stores the gist of the list, and when you remember, it rebuilds the details from that gist.

Sleep is part of studying

Much of the work of making a memory last happens after the learning session is over, especially overnight. Cramming late and cutting sleep short undermines the very process that stores what was studied.

Why it matters for learningMemory is teamwork that takes time

  1. Spread it outSeveral shorter sessions, with nights in between, give memories time to settle.
  2. Sleep on itSleep after learning helps move new memories into longer-term storage.
  3. Practise recallingEach recall rebuilds the memory and makes it stronger. Check it against the source, because recall can also drift.
