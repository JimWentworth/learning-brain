In 1983, psychologist Howard Gardner proposed multiple intelligences: separate abilities, each with its own brain basis. It was a generous idea that challenged a single number for intelligence, and teachers embraced it.

But the theory makes testable claims.

1983Howard Gardner proposes multiple intelligences
Go togetherscores on the “separate” abilities tend to correlate
No distinctbrain systems found for each intelligence
2006, 2023reviews: the theory lacks empirical support

The testSeparate, or together?

If the intelligences are separate, scores on them should be largely independent. Studies found the opposite: they tend to go together. And researchers have not found distinct brain systems for each one.

In reviews in 2006 and 2023, Lynn Waterhouse concluded that the theory lacks empirical support, and that studies of teaching to the intelligences were not done by standard scientific methods. Even so, the idea remains popular in teacher training.

Gardner’s own lineNot learning styles

Gardner himself has drawn a line. He says multiple intelligences are not learning styles, and that matching lessons to a student’s intelligence is a misreading of his work.

The grain of truthMany representations

People do have different strengths and interests. And showing an idea in more than one way, a graph, an equation, a story, can deepen understanding for everyone.

For teaching

Choose representations because the content needs them, not to match student types. Value many kinds of talent without sorting students into boxes. And put planning time into methods with strong evidence. Share this tip with students: use many representations because the content needs them, not because you are a type.
