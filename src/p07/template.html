Hearing with your eyes

Speech perception isn’t just hearing. The brain combines the sound with what it sees on the speaker’s face, so strongly that a mismatched face can change the sound you hear. That matters in every lecture hall.

Read my lips

Each word is shown as three mouth shapes, one per sound, with no audio. Which word is it? Sometimes the honest answer is that you can’t tell.

About 10 minutes, plus an optional live demo

Run it in class

  1. Run the student version with the class voting on each word.
  2. Live demo: silently mouth “bat”, “pat” and “mat” in random order and ask the class to call out which. Then mouth “fan” and “pan”.
  3. Discuss which sounds the lips show (where the mouth closes) and which they hide (whether the voice is on).
  4. Connect to your room: where do you stand when you speak? What happens when you talk while writing on the board?

Ask the room

  • Why could you tell “fan” from “pan”, but not “bat” from “pat”?
  • What does hearing add that the lips can’t?
  • Who in this room depends most on seeing your face, or on captions?

Connect it to your course

Face the room when you speak, don’t talk while writing on the board, use a microphone when you have one, and turn on captions for video. Seeing the speaker helps everyone, and it helps most when the room is noisy.

Good to know

The McGurk effect is easiest to experience in a dubbed video, and many demonstration videos exist online. Watch with your eyes closed, then open.

In 1976, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald dubbed the sound of someone saying “ba” onto a film of a woman’s face saying “ga”. Adults watching it reported hearing “da”: neither the sound nor the lips, but a blend. With their eyes closed, they heard “ba” correctly.

The McGurk effect shows that speech perception combines what we hear and what we see. The brain doesn’t keep them separate; it makes one best guess from both.

In noiseThe face fills in what the ear misses

William Sumby and Irwin Pollack tested how well people understood spoken words in noise, with and without seeing the speaker’s face. Seeing the face helped across noise levels, and because there is so much more to recover when the sound is poor, the face added the most in the noisiest conditions.

What lips showWhere, not whether

The lips show where in the mouth a sound is made: “p”, “b” and “m” all close the lips; “f” and “v” put the teeth on the lip. They can’t show whether the voice is on, which is the difference between “p” and “b”. Hearing supplies that. That is why the activity above has words you can read and words you can’t.

CaptionsA large literature, with caveats

In a 2015 review, Morton Ann Gernsbacher listed more than 100 studies reporting that same-language captions improve comprehension of, attention to and memory for videos. Captions are especially helpful for people watching in a second language, people learning to read, and people who are deaf or hard of hearing. Critics note that much of this evidence comes from those groups, and that well-controlled studies with typical hearing students are fewer. For many students, though, captions are essential, and they are required for accessibility in most educational settings.
