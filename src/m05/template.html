In a classic 1972 study by John Bransford and Marcia Johnson, people heard a passage written in deliberately vague terms: sort the items into groups; one pile may be enough; mistakes can be costly. Most found it hard to follow, and harder to remember.

Then some were told the topic: washing clothes. People told before the passage understood it and remembered far more. People told afterwards did no better than people never told.

TimingKnowledge helps only while you learn

In a version of the study with a picture, people who saw it before hearing the passage recalled about 8 of its 14 ideas. People who saw it afterwards recalled under 4, the same as people who never saw it. Knowledge helps only if it is active while you learn. An organized body of knowledge like this is called a schema.

ExpertsPatterns, not better memories

Chess masters shown a board from a real game for five seconds could rebuild it almost perfectly; weaker players placed only a few pieces. But when the same pieces were scattered at random, the masters’ advantage nearly vanished, as William Chase and Herbert Simon reported in 1973. Their memory wasn’t better in general. It was built on patterns they already knew.

In the brainA schema speeds up consolidation

In 2007, Dorothy Tse, Richard Morris and colleagues spent many sessions teaching rats where six flavours were hidden in an arena. Once that map was in place, the rats learned a new flavour’s location in a single trial, and within two days the memory no longer needed the hippocampus. Rats trained in an arena where the layout kept changing couldn’t do this. Later work from the same lab pointed to regions of the cortex, including the front of the brain, as where the new information was being added.

A cautionWhen the frame is wrong

Prior knowledge helps when it is accurate. When it is wrong, new material gets bent to fit the old schema, and misconceptions can survive instruction.

New learning needs something to hold on to

Before new material, bring to mind what students already know: a question, a familiar example, the big picture first. Give the topic and structure before the details, not after. Check prior knowledge for errors, not just gaps. And build courses so early ideas become the frame for later ones.
