Nearly 300 people wrote a short, eight-sentence story. A third worked alone. The others could see one AI story idea, or choose from up to five. Everyone wrote their own story, and none of them were professional writers. Six hundred evaluators judged the stories. Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser reported the results in 2024.

With access to five AI ideas, stories were rated about 8% more novel and 9% more useful, meaning better crafted and closer to publishable. The biggest gains went to the least creative writers. AI levelled the field, as it did for the consultants in topic 3.

SamenessEach writer gained; the collection lost variety

But across the group, the AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other. The AI ideas pulled writers toward similar stories.

The authors call this a social dilemma. Each person has a reason to use AI, but if everyone starts from the same suggestions, the shared pool of ideas narrows. The work drifts toward the same place.

Make sameness visible

Have students generate ideas alone first, then use AI to extend or challenge them. Reward difference: ask how a submission departs from what the AI suggested. In group work, compare ideas across the class, to make sameness visible. And treat the AI’s first suggestion as the obvious answer to beat, not the one to use.

Why it matters for learningAI can lift each person’s ideas. Keeping them different is up to us.
