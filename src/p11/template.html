Graphs that fool the eye

Graphs are read by the visual system, and it judges some things far better than others. Positions along a scale are easy; areas and angles are hard; a cut-off axis can make a small difference look huge.

Run it in class

  1. Show each chart in part 1 and ask everyone to write down their estimate of “B is how many times A?” before revealing.
  2. Compare the spread of answers for bars, circles and pie slices. Which were people most accurate on?
  3. In part 2, show the cut-off axis chart first and take a vote on how big the difference is. Then show the full axis.
  4. Look at a chart from your own slides or a textbook. Which comparison does it ask the eye to make? Could position do it better?

Ask the room

  • Which chart type gave the most accurate estimates? Why?
  • Did knowing the axis was cut off change your impression?
  • When is a cut-off axis honest, and when is it misleading?

Connect it to your course

Put the comparison that matters on a common scale, as position or length, and label the values. Start bar charts at zero. Use pies and bubbles only when rough proportions are enough.

Good to know

There is no single right answer to “how big does it look”; the point is to compare your impression of the two charts.

In 1984, the statisticians William Cleveland and Robert McGill asked a simple question: when a chart encodes a number as a position, a length, an angle or an area, how accurately can people read it? Their experiments produced a ranking that still shapes chart design.

Judgements of position along a common scale were the most accurate, then length, then angle and slope, with area judgements last.

Why bars beat piesDifferent judgements

A bar chart asks the eye to compare the ends of bars against a shared axis. A pie chart asks it to compare angles or areas, which the visual system judges less accurately. That doesn’t make pies useless for showing rough shares of a whole, but it makes them a poor choice when the size of a difference matters.

The cut-off axisA small gap made large

Michael Correll, Enrico Bertini and Steven Franconeri tested how people judge differences when a chart’s y-axis doesn’t start at zero. Truncated axes made differences look bigger, across chart types, and the effect persisted even with designs that explicitly marked the truncation. They suggest choosing an axis that matches the size of difference that actually matters, rather than following a single rule.

For learningCharts are read by perception first

Students form an impression of a chart before they read its numbers, and that impression is hard to undo. Design for the comparison you want them to make, and teach them to check the axis before they trust the picture.
