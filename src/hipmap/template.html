Some cells deep in the brain fire only in one particular place. Together they make a map, and that map can hold far more than streets.

In 1971, John O’Keefe was recording from single cells in the hippocampus of rats as they explored. One cell fired only when the rat was in one particular spot. When the rat moved elsewhere, that cell went quiet and other cells fired instead.

He called them place cells. Each covers a different patch of space, and together they work like a map: the pattern of active cells shows where the animal is. In 2014, O’Keefe shared the Nobel Prize with May-Britt and Edvard Moser, who found grid cells in the neighbouring entorhinal cortex.

    The human mapLondon’s taxi drivers

    To get a licence, London taxi drivers must learn “the Knowledge”: about 25,000 streets and thousands of landmarks. Training takes three to four years, and many trainees never pass.

    Earlier studies by Eleanor Maguire found that qualified drivers had more grey matter at the back of the hippocampus than other people. But were they born that way? To find out, Maguire and Katherine Woollett scanned 79 trainees before and after training, along with 31 people who didn’t train.

    Change in grey matter at the back of the hippocampus after training
    GroupBack of the hippocampus
    39 trainees who qualifiedMore grey matter than before
    Trainees who failedNo such change
    People who never trainedNo such change

    Before training, the groups looked the same, so the change came from learning the city. There was a cost too: the qualified drivers did worse than the others at recalling complex visual information.

    Borrowing the mapThe method of loci

    An old story tells of the Greek poet Simonides, who left a banquet moments before the roof fell in. He could name the victims by remembering where each had been sitting. Places, it seems, are good hooks for memory.

    In the method of loci (“loci” is Latin for places), you picture things you want to remember at points along a route you know well, then walk the route in your mind to recall them. In a study of top memory competitors, Maguire and colleagues found they were not more intelligent and their brains were not built differently. Nine of the ten used this method, and their scans showed spatial-memory areas at work, including the hippocampus.

    Ordinary people can learn it. In a 2017 study, people practised for six weeks and went from recalling 26 words of a 72-word list to 62. Four months later, they still recalled far more than before.

    Try itBuild a memory palace

    You will learn two lists of eight words for the same time each. Learn the first by reading. Learn the second by placing each word in a room of your own home. Then compare how many you recall.

    Why it matters for learningUse the map you have

    1. Hang it on a structureLink new ideas to something you know well, such as a familiar route, the order of a process, or the layout of a diagram.
    2. Walk it againRecall by walking through the structure in your head. Each walk is practice at pulling the memories back out.
    3. Give it timeDeep learning reshapes the brain slowly. London’s taxi drivers needed three to four years.
