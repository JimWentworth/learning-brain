Phones and attention Guard your attention; it is where learning happens

Your phone may be the most powerful distraction ever built to fit in a pocket. The clearest costs come from checking, switching and alerts, not from the phone just sitting there.

Read the transcript

In a simulated lecture, reported by Faria Sana, Tina Weston and Nicholas Cepeda in 2013, students who multitasked on a laptop scored 11% lower on a comprehension test. Students who simply sat where they could see a multitasking classmate scored 17% lower, and most did not think they were distracted. Sana said that gap could turn a B+ into a B−.

Notifications cost you too. At Florida State University, Cary Stothart and colleagues had 166 students do an attention task while the researchers secretly called or texted the students’ own phones. Those who got a notification made more mistakes, even without checking it.

AlertsAs costly as using the phone

The cost of a notification was comparable to the cost of actually using a phone for a call or a text. You don’t have to pick it up to lose some of your attention.

Mere presenceA finding that didn’t hold

You may have heard that just having your phone on the desk drains your brain. A 2017 study by Adrian Ward and colleagues reported that. But a pre-registered direct replication did not find the effect, and a study by Matthias Hartmann at the University of Bern and colleagues found no evidence for it either. So the clearer lesson is about use and interruptions, not mere presence: the real costs come from checking, switching between tasks, and alerts.

A cautionLab tasks, but the same direction

The laptop studies used simulated lectures, and the notification study used a simple lab task. But they point the same way, and the fix costs nothing.

Try this

Turn notifications off while you study. Close extra tabs and apps. In class, sit where you can’t see other people’s screens. And check your phone in planned breaks, not whenever it buzzes.
