Learning a language as an adult

Adults have one big advantage over children: they can choose how to study. The catch is that the methods that feel most effective, rereading lists and highlighting, are not the ones that work best.

About 10 minutes, or split across two classes

Run it in class

  1. Students do the activity on their own devices.
  2. For a stronger effect, stop after the practice phase and run the final test at the start of the next class.
  3. Ask which words felt better learned during practice, and which were remembered in the test.
  4. Discuss how students actually study vocabulary and terminology: rereading lists, flashcards that they flip too soon, or genuine recall?

Ask the room

  • Which practice felt more effective at the time?
  • Which words did you remember in the final test?
  • How could you turn your own study of terms into recall rather than rereading?

Connect it to your course

Give students recall practice with key terms: low-stakes quizzes, flashcards used properly (answer before flipping), and returning to earlier terms in later weeks. The Learning Brain series covers retrieval and spacing in depth.

Good to know

With only a few minutes between practice and test, the difference can be small. In the original study the gap was large a week later.

In a study published in Science, Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger had university students learn 40 Swahili–English word pairs, such as mashua–boat, over several alternating study and test periods. Some students kept being tested on all the words; others kept studying them but were tested less.

A week later, students who had been repeatedly tested on every pair recalled about 80%. Those who weren’t recalled about 35%. And the students seemed unaware of this advantage.

Why recall worksPractising the skill you’ll need

Using a word means retrieving it: finding “boat” when you see mashua, or finding mashua when you want to say boat. Rereading practises recognising; recalling practises retrieving. Karpicke described remembering as a skill that gets better with practice.

Feeling versus learningWhy students choose the wrong method

Rereading a list makes it feel familiar, and familiarity feels like learning. Recall is harder and produces mistakes, so it feels worse in the moment, even though it leads to more learning. Students need to know that, or they will drift back to rereading.

Beyond wordsUse, input and time

Vocabulary is only part of a language. Topic 2 covers how words build up from many encounters in context, and topic 8 how much of the vocabulary listeners need to know to follow speech. Spacing practice over days, rather than cramming, adds further benefit; the Learning Brain series covers spacing and retrieval in detail.

For learningIt isn’t only for language courses

Every discipline asks students to learn a vocabulary. The same principle applies to anatomy, legal terms or chemical names: practise retrieving them, not just reading them.
