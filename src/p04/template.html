Only the centre is sharp

You feel you see a whole page at once. In fact only a tiny patch is sharp at any moment, and your eyes jump several times a second to build the picture. That has consequences for every slide you show.

See like your fovea

Only a small circle of the text will be sharp, roughly the size of the sharp centre of your vision. Move it with the pointer (or arrow keys) to find the answer. Two rounds, timed.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Students use their own devices; this one needs a pointer, finger or arrow keys for each person.
  2. Run round 1 (dense paragraph) and round 2 (labelled layout). Students note both times.
  3. Collect a few times on the board. Ask why round 2 was quicker.
  4. Show one of your own slides. Ask: where would your eyes land first? How many jumps to find the key point?

Ask the room

  • What did you have to do to find the answer in round 1?
  • What about the layout in round 2 let you jump straight to it?
  • If students are reading this slide, can they also listen to me?

Connect it to your course

Every word on a slide costs eye movements, a few per line. Put the key point where the eye lands, use labels and short lines, and avoid making students read dense text while you talk.

Good to know

Round 2 is easier partly because it is laid out with labels, which is the point. The two rounds aren’t a controlled experiment.

The fovea, the small pit at the centre of the retina, covers only about the central 2 degrees of what you see. Outside it, detail falls away fast. To read, the eyes must keep moving the fovea onto the next few letters.

Reading is a series of brief stops, called fixations, usually 200 to 250 milliseconds each, separated by fast jumps called saccades that last 20 to 50 milliseconds. Keith Rayner, who spent decades measuring this, compared reading to a slide show of short segments of text.

~2°of the visual field falls on the fovea, the sharp centre
200–250 msa typical fixation when reading
20–50 msa typical saccade, the jump between fixations
3–4 / 14–15letters to the left and right that a skilled reader takes in per fixation

The perceptual spanA small window that moves

Using displays that change with each eye movement, researchers measured how much text a reader actually uses during one fixation. For skilled readers of English, it is about three or four letters to the left of where they are looking and 14 or 15 letters to the right. The rest of the line is a blur of shapes and spaces that helps plan the next jump.

Why it feels wholeThe brain stitches it together

We don’t experience a page as a small sharp spot jumping about, because the brain combines what it took in across fixations, and because we seldom notice what we didn’t look at. The activity above makes the hidden process visible, in simplified form: real readers also get some information from just outside the fovea.

For learningEvery word on a slide costs a jump

A slide full of sentences takes many fixations to read, and while students read they are not looking at you or the diagram. A slide with short labelled lines lets the eye go straight to what matters. The Digital Learning series looks at the evidence on reading slides aloud and on extra text.
