Richard Mayer’s theory of multimedia learning starts from three ideas. We take in information through two channels, one for words and one for pictures. Each channel has limited capacity. And learning happens when we actively connect the two.

Across decades of experiments, students who learned from words and pictures did better on transfer tests, which ask them to use what they learned in a new way, than students who got words alone.

The diagram does not add new facts. It shows how the parts connect, which is the hard part to build from words alone.

Which picturesPictures that explain

Not just any picture helps. The useful ones show how something works or how parts relate: a diagram, a process, a graph. Decorative images do not count, and they can get in the way (topic 2).

Not learning stylesEveryone benefits

This is not learning styles. Every student benefits from words and pictures together, because the content has both a verbal and a visual side.

A cautionA boundary

The effect is stronger for beginners than for experts. Students who already know a topic can build the picture in their heads; beginners need it on the page. And most of these experiments used short lessons on how things work.

For teaching

Pair your key explanations with a simple diagram that shows how the parts connect. Put labels on the diagram, close to what they describe. Give beginners more visuals than advanced students. And share this tip with students: sketch a diagram next to your notes.
