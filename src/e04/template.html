In 2009, Lindsey Richland, Nate Kornell and Liche Sean Kao gave students questions about a science passage before they read it. Students got almost all of the pretest questions wrong.

But after reading, they remembered the pretested information better than students who simply read the passage for longer. The benefit held even for the questions they had answered incorrectly.

The reviewWith one key condition

A 2023 review by Steven Pan and Shana Carpenter found the pretesting effect across texts, videos and lectures, with one key condition: students need a chance to study the correct answers afterwards.

WhyA wrong guess points the way

A pretest tells students what matters, so they pay attention when it comes up. And searching memory, even unsuccessfully, primes the connections that the right answer will attach to. It also changes how students see errors: a mistake on a pretest costs nothing, and becomes the start of learning, not a judgment.

A cautionUngraded, followed up

Pretests should be ungraded, and followed by the material that answers them. Students tend to dislike guessing, so explain why you are doing it. And the clearest benefit is for the pretested information itself, so ask about the ideas that matter most.

For teaching

Start a unit or a video with two or three questions students can’t yet answer. Don’t grade them. Then teach, and return to the questions at the end. Share this tip with students: guess first, then check the answer.
