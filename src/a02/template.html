Students studied a list of words in one computer file, then a second list in another. When they saved the first file before moving on, they remembered more of the second list. Benjamin Storm and Sean Stone reported this in 2015. Offloading the first list freed room for the next.

Psychologists call this cognitive offloading: using the world, a note, a device, or now an AI, to lower the demand on your own head. It’s as old as writing, and often smart.

The costBetter now, less later

Offloading has a cost. In 2021, Sandra Grinschgl, Frank Papenmeier and Hauke Meyerhoff had people copy colour patterns, choosing how often to look back at the model instead of holding it in mind. People who offloaded more performed better in the moment and remembered less afterwards, across three experiments of 172 people each.

The cost wasn’t fixed. When people who offloaded as much as possible knew a memory test was coming, they almost entirely made up for it. Intending to learn changed what stayed. And Storm and Stone’s saving benefit vanished when people were told the saved file might not be there: offloading only frees the mind when the store is trusted.

HeadlinesSeparate the pattern from the hype

One famous 2011 claim, that hard questions make us think of searching the internet, failed to replicate in a large 2018 project led by Colin Camerer. The broad pattern of offloading is solid; some striking headlines aren’t. These are lab tasks with lists and patterns, and generative AI can offload more than memory: it can offload the reasoning itself.

Say what must stay in their heads

Decide what students must hold in their own heads, and say so. Let them offload the rest, and tell them when a test is coming, so they learn on purpose. And when AI does a step, have them restate it in their own words.
