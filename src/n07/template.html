The evidenceDouble-blind

In 1995, Mark Wolraich and colleagues pooled 23 double-blind studies in the Journal of the American Medical Association. Children got either sugar or a sugar-free placebo, and no one watching knew which. The conclusion: sugar did not affect children’s behaviour or cognitive performance.

Why we see itExpectations

A 1994 study by Daniel Hoover and Richard Milich offers a clue. Thirty-five boys, described by their mothers as sugar-sensitive, all received a sugar-free drink. Mothers who were told their son had sugar rated him as more hyperactive, and behaved differently toward him. Expectations shape what we see. And settings matter too: parties and holidays are exciting, with or without the cake.

A cautionSmall effects not ruled out

The authors noted that a small effect, or effects in a few sensitive children, could not be ruled out. And too much sugar still harms health. It just does not explain the party.

For instructorsThe lesson travels

When we expect a student to struggle or misbehave, we tend to see it, and we may treat them differently.

For teaching

Before you explain a student’s behaviour, look at the situation, not a single cause. Notice when your expectations are doing the observing. And base judgments on evidence you can check. Share this tip with students: notice the setting, not just the snack.
