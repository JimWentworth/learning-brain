The Parietal Lobe Space, attention and number

At the top and back of the brain, the parietal lobe keeps track of where things are, steers attention, and gives us a feel for how many. Even our sense of number is laid out in space.

In 1978, the Milan neurologists Edoardo Bisiach and Claudio Luzzatti asked two patients, known as I.G. and N.V., to imagine standing in the Piazza del Duomo, the city’s cathedral square, which both knew well. Picturing themselves at one end, facing the cathedral, they listed buildings and shops, but almost all were on their right.

Then the patients imagined standing on the cathedral steps, looking back across the square. Now they named the buildings they had left out, and left out the ones they had just described. Nothing was wrong with their memory of the square. Whatever fell on the left of their mental picture simply dropped out.

SpaceWhen half the world goes missing

Both Milan patients had damage to the right side of the brain. The condition they had, unilateral neglect, most often follows damage to the right parietal lobe, although nearby areas can be involved too. Similar damage on the left usually causes milder, shorter-lived problems on the right side.

People with left neglect may eat only the food on the right half of the plate, shave only one side of the face, or copy only the right half of a drawing. Asked to mark the middle of a line, they put the mark well to the right. They are often unaware that anything is missing. The Milan study showed that neglect affects more than what people see: it affects the space of their imagination too.

AttentionA spotlight you can steer

Neglect is a problem of attention, not of eyesight. In 2002, Maurizio Corbetta and Gordon Shulman described two partly separate attention networks. A dorsal network, which includes the intraparietal sulcus (a deep groove along the parietal lobe) and an area in the frontal lobe that controls eye movements, points attention where we choose. A ventral network, mainly on the right, acts as a circuit breaker, pulling attention toward something unexpected. Both are disrupted in neglect.

Because attention is limited, we can look straight at something and still miss it. In 2005, Gustav Kuhn and Benjamin Tatler filmed people’s eye movements as Kuhn made a cigarette “vanish” by dropping it, in full view. Only 2 of the 20 viewers saw it fall. Where people were looking at the moment of the drop made little difference. What mattered was where their attention was.

NumberA sense of how many

Glance at three coins and you know there are three without counting. In 1949, E. L. Kaufman and colleagues named this subitizing, from the Latin for sudden. Up to about four items, each extra item adds only a little time. Beyond four, each one adds much more, because we have to count, or settle for an estimate.

The intraparietal sulcus is central to this sense of number. In 2004, Manuela Piazza, Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues showed people the same number of dots again and again, until the response there died down. When the number changed, the response bounced back, and more so for bigger changes. Later work found the same region responds to number whether it is shown as dots, digits or number words.

The number lineFrom squashed to straight

We also lay numbers out in space, with small numbers on one side and large ones on the other. In 2003, Robert Siegler and John Opfer asked children and adults to mark numbers on a line running from 0 to 1,000. Nearly all second graders spread the small numbers out and crowded the large ones together, a pattern that matches a logarithmic curve. Some put 150 closer to 1,000 than to 0. About half of fourth graders did the same, while sixth graders and adults spaced numbers evenly.

The same shift happens at different ages for different ranges: between preschool and kindergarten for 0 to 10, between kindergarten and second grade for 0 to 100, and between second and fourth grade for 0 to 1,000. Children’s number-line estimates go along with their maths achievement.

    PracticeBoard games straighten the line

    Where do children get an even number line? One source is playing games where numbers march in a straight line. In 2008, Geetha Ramani and Robert Siegler had preschoolers from low-income families play a simple race game on a board with squares numbered 1 to 10. After four 15-minute sessions, the children improved at counting, naming numbers, comparing which number is bigger, and placing numbers on a number line. Children who played the same game with coloured squares instead of numbers did not improve.

    The layout matters. In a related study, the same game played on a circular board did not produce the same gains. A straight row of numbers gives children a picture of numbers that are evenly spaced, each one step further along than the last.

    Look where I’m pointing

    Attention decides what gets learned. A learner looking at a busy diagram may be attending to the wrong part, just like the viewers who missed the cigarette. Pointing, highlighting and removing distractions help steer attention to what matters.
