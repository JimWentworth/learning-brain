The mapAn inner positioning system

John O’Keefe and Jonathan Dostrovsky reported these place cells in 1971. In 2005, May-Britt and Edvard Moser’s lab found grid cells next door, in the entorhinal cortex. Each grid cell fires at many spots laid out in a repeating triangular pattern, like the lines of graph paper. Together, place cells and grid cells work like an inner GPS. The three shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

AdultsThe map can grow

Trainee London taxi drivers spend 3 to 4 years learning the city’s 25,000 streets, known as “the Knowledge”, and only about half pass. Katherine Woollett and Eleanor Maguire scanned 79 trainees and 31 people who weren’t training, before and after. The trainees who qualified gained grey matter in the back of the hippocampus. Those who failed, and the comparison group, showed no such change.

The methodA memory palace

An ancient memory method, the method of loci, borrows the map. You picture each item at a spot along a route you know well, then walk the route in your mind to bring the items back. In 2017, Martin Dresler and colleagues trained people with ordinary memories for 30 minutes a day. After 40 days, they went from recalling an average of 26 words out of 72 to 62. Four months later, without more training, they still recalled 22 more words than at the start.

Try itBuild a memory palace

Two rounds of 10 words, shown one at a time. In the first, remember them any way you like. In the second, you’ll place each word at a spot in a house. Each round ends with a 15-second easy task, then you type what you remember. About 4 minutes.

Beyond spaceA map for ideas

The map may reach beyond physical space. In 2016, Alexandra Constantinescu, Jill O’Reilly and Timothy Behrens taught people a set of cartoon birds that differed in neck length and leg length. Changing the bird felt like moving through a two-dimensional “bird space”. As people moved through it in their minds, brain scans showed a grid-like signal, much like the one seen during navigation.

Some cautionFinding is not understanding

Memory palaces are strong for lists and sequences, such as the order of steps, names or dates. They don’t build understanding: a place helps you find an idea, but doesn’t explain it. And findings about the size of brain areas come from small groups, so treat them as a sign that the adult brain can change, not as a recipe.

Give ideas a consistent place

Keep the same board layout from week to week. Draw diagrams with stable positions and come back to them. Show a map of the course, so each topic sits somewhere. And let students lay out what they know in space, as a concept map or a timeline on the wall.
