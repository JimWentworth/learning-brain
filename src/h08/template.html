Illusions of learning Feeling fluent is not the same as knowing

Rereading your notes feels productive. The words look familiar, and familiar feels like learned. But recognizing something is not the same as being able to recall it in an exam.

In a survey of 177 college students, reported by Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger in 2009, 84% said they reread their notes or textbook, and 55% called it their top strategy. Only 11% said they tested themselves, and just 1% ranked that first.

Rereading builds fluency: the material feels easy because you have just seen it. In one classic experiment, by Roediger and Karpicke, students who reread a passage did slightly better five minutes later. But after a week, students who had practised recalling it remembered 56%, against 42% for the rereaders.

MixingThe same illusion

Spacing and mixing show the same illusion. Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork had students learn painters’ styles with each artist’s paintings in a block, or with artists mixed together. Mixing worked better for most of them. Yet 78% believed blocking had helped more, even after their own test showed the opposite. A large online replication in 2014, by Peter Verkoeijen and Samantha Bouwmeester, found a similar advantage for mixing.

A cautionRereading isn’t useless

These are mostly lab tasks with short materials. And rereading is not useless: a first read is how you take the material in. The problem is relying on it to check what you know.

Try this

After reading, close the book and write down what you remember. Quiz yourself with questions, not highlights. Mix topics, and space sessions across days. And when something feels easy, test it before you trust it.
