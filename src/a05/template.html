In Harvard’s largest introductory physics course, 194 students took two lessons each. One week they learned in an active-learning class; the other week, at home with an AI tutor. Then they swapped. Greg Kestin, Kelly Miller and colleagues reported the results in 2025.

The class was not a weak comparison. It already used research-based active learning.

194students in a crossover experiment, each taught both ways
More than doublethe median learning gain with the AI tutor
4.5 vs 3.5median scores out of 6, up from 2.75 before the lesson
49 minutesmedian time with the tutor, against a 60-minute class

The designNot an off-the-shelf chatbot

The tutor was built on learning science. It worked one step at a time and made students do the thinking. It managed cognitive load, gave immediate feedback, and let students set their own pace. It had the correct solutions built in, to keep it accurate. Students also said they felt more engaged and more motivated.

Set beside the previous topic, the lesson is clear. The same technology can harm learning, or double it. Design decides.

A cautionNotable, not a general law

This was one course, two topics and short-term tests, with highly motivated students. The comparison class was already strong, which makes the gain notable, but not a general law.

Choose a tutor that asks before it tells

If you build or choose an AI tutor, require that it asks before it tells. Load it with your own correct solutions. Use it for first exposure to content, and spend class time on discussion and harder problems. And tell students why the tutor holds back the answer, so they don’t route around it.
