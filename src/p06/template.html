Gestalt: how the brain groups a page

Before you read a single word, your brain has already decided what goes with what: things that are close, alike, boxed together or connected. Every slide and handout is grouped this way, whether you planned it or not.

Run it in class

  1. Project the student version. For each layout, count “one, two, three” and have everyone call out what they see as grouped.
  2. After each one, name the principle shown: proximity, similarity, common region, connection or closure.
  3. At the last layout, ask which version of the labelled diagram is easier to read, and why.
  4. Then show one of your own slides and ask: what will students group together here? Is that what you meant?

Ask the room

  • Which layouts did everyone agree on immediately?
  • Where did spacing and colour pull in different directions?
  • On your last handout, what would a reader group together?

Connect it to your course

Use grouping deliberately: put labels next to what they label, leave space between unrelated items, and use boxes or shading to show what belongs together.

Good to know

There is no single right answer for every layout; the “most people” answers come from long-standing demonstrations, and individuals vary.

In 1912, Max Wertheimer published a paper on apparent motion that is widely seen as the start of Gestalt psychology. He and his colleagues went on to describe how the visual system organises a scene into groups and figures before we think about it: things that are close together, alike, enclosed together, or connected tend to be seen as belonging together.

A century later, a major review by Johan Wagemans and colleagues concluded that the questions the Gestalt psychologists raised, about how structure emerges in perception, still drive research today.

AutomaticGrouping happens first

You don’t choose to see the dots in the first layout as columns. Grouping is part of how the visual system builds a scene, and it shapes what we notice and compare. When two cues conflict, such as spacing and colour, the stronger one tends to win.

On the pageLayout makes a claim

Whatever is close together on a slide will be read as related. A caption that sits nearer to the wrong picture, or a label floating between two parts, sends a message you didn’t mean to send.

The evidenceKeep related things together

Research on multimedia learning tests this directly. When a diagram’s labels are placed next to the parts, rather than in a separate key, learners don’t have to search back and forth. Paul Ginns’s 2006 meta-analysis of this split-attention effect found a large average benefit for integrated designs (d = 0.85), and a 2018 meta-analysis by Noah Schroeder and Ada Cenkci also supported placing related words and pictures close together.
