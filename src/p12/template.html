Good to know

The topics in this series point the same way. Perception is the brain’s best guess, built from context and expectation. It is sharp only in a small area and only for what we attend to. It groups, fills in and judges relative to surroundings. And experts and beginners literally see differently.

None of that is a flaw to fix in students. It’s how vision and hearing work for everyone. Teaching works better when it works with it.

BeforePlan what students will see

Put labels next to what they label and keep unrelated things apart, since layout tells the eye what goes together. Check text contrast with a tool, at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text, and don’t rely on colour alone. For comparisons that matter, use positions or lengths on a shared axis, and start bar charts at zero. Cut words from slides: every word costs an eye movement.

DuringGuide attention

Before you show an image, say what it is meant to show. Point to the part you are talking about, and build complex diagrams up in steps. When something changes, say so; unannounced changes are easily missed. Face the room when you speak, and use the microphone and captions.

AfterCheck and train

Ask students to describe what they saw before you interpret it; their reading may differ from yours. For the distinctions that experts see at a glance, give many short, mixed examples with feedback.

A cautionPrinciples, not a formula

Most of the evidence in this series comes from perception labs and multimedia learning studies, often with short lessons. The effects are real but vary by learner and setting. Use these as defaults and check them against what your students actually see.
