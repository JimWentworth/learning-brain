Priming Warming up the network

What you have just seen or thought about quietly shapes what comes next. Related ideas get a head start, and new information sticks better when something is already switched on for it to attach to.

Read this word: doctor. Now this one: nurse. In 1971, David Meyer and Roger Schvaneveldt asked 12 high-school students to decide, as fast as they could, whether two strings of letters were both real words. When the words were related, like doctor and nurse, answers came about 85 milliseconds faster than for unrelated pairs like bread and door.

That head start is called semantic priming. It suggests that knowledge isn’t stored as a list. It’s a web of related ideas, and touching one part of the web warms up its neighbours.

The webCloser to the threshold

One leading explanation, described by Allan Collins and Elizabeth Loftus in 1975, is spreading activation. When one idea is activated, some of that activity spreads along the links to related ideas. They are not fully switched on, but they are closer to the threshold, so when one of them actually arrives, it takes less to tip it over.

Something similar happens when you meet the same thing twice. The second time, the brain does less work for the same result: in brain scans, the response to a repeated item is smaller. This can happen even when you don’t remember the first encounter.

Prime the lessonSomething to attach to

Priming matters for learning because new information needs something already active to attach to. In 1972, John Bransford and Marcia Johnson had people listen to a vague paragraph describing a procedure: sort things into groups, go somewhere else if you have to, and so on. One group was told the topic first: washing clothes. They recalled about twice as much as people who heard no topic. People told the topic only afterwards did no better than those never told at all. The words were identical. What changed was what was already active when they arrived.

Some cautionWhat priming can’t do

Some of the most famous priming claims have not held up. In 1996, John Bargh and colleagues reported that students who unscrambled sentences containing words about old age walked more slowly as they left the lab. In 2012, Stéphane Doyen and colleagues repeated the study with twice as many people and infrared timing, and found no effect, except when the experimenters timing the walk had been led to expect slower walking. The effects that hold up are smaller and more specific, like the head start for related words.

Questions firstEven a wrong guess helps

Priming a topic can be as simple as asking questions about it first. In 2009, Nate Kornell, Matthew Hays and Robert Bjork had people try to guess an answer for 8 seconds before studying it for 5. They remembered more than people who simply studied the same material for all 13 seconds, even though their guesses were almost always wrong.

Warm the network first

Before teaching something new, show where the session is headed, ask learners what they already know about it, or pose a question they can’t answer yet. Priming won’t do the learning for you, but the new idea lands on a network that’s already warm.

  1. Show where it’s headedGive the topic, an overview or a picture before the details.
  2. Activate what’s thereAsk learners to recall what they already know about the topic.
