How long should a video be? Short is not shallow. Short is focused.

You record a 50-minute lecture and post it online. How much of it will students actually watch? One of the largest studies of educational video suggests: less than you might hope.

In 2014, Philip Guo, Juho Kim and Rob Rubin analyzed 6.9 million video-watching sessions from four edX courses: 862 videos and almost 128,000 students.

Their clearest finding: shorter videos were much more engaging. Engagement dropped sharply after about six minutes. For longer videos, many students stopped well before the end.

StyleWhat held attention

Style mattered too. Videos that mixed slides with the instructor’s face were more engaging than slides alone. Hand-drawn tablet explanations held attention. And instructors who spoke fairly fast, with enthusiasm, kept students watching. Even well-produced recordings of classroom lectures were less engaging than videos made for online viewing.

SegmentingShort videos are segments

This connects to the segmenting principle: people learn better when a lesson is broken into learner-paced parts. Short videos are segments, with a natural pause between them.

Your plan

A cautionWatching is not learning

This study measured watching, not learning. It came from massive open online courses, whose students differ from those in a for-credit class. Still, the pattern fits what we know about attention.

For teaching

Plan each video around one idea, and aim for six minutes or less. Split long lectures into a series of short ones, with a question or activity between them. Show your face at key moments, and talk to students, not to the slides. Share this tip with students: watch in short, focused chunks.
