Wise feedback High standards, and belief that students can reach them. Say both.

Critical feedback can be read two ways: my teacher thinks I can do better, or my teacher thinks people like me can’t do this. For students who expect to be stereotyped, that ambiguity is real.

Read the transcript

In a middle school study by David Yeager, Valerie Purdie-Vaughns, Julio Garcia, Geoffrey Cohen and colleagues, seventh graders wrote essays about a personal hero, and their teachers marked them up. Some students also got a handwritten note: “I’m giving you these comments because I have very high expectations, and I know that you can reach them.” Others got a neutral note.

Far more of the African American students who got the high-expectations note chose to revise their essay, and the difference was largest among those who most distrusted school.

Required revisionBetter drafts, too

In a second study, revision was required. 88% of African American students with the note improved their scores, compared with 34% without. White students also benefited a little, but that difference was not significant.

Two things at onceThe bar, and the belief

The note did two things at once. It held the bar high, and it said: I believe you can reach it. Earlier research found that praise alone, or high standards alone, worked less well.

A cautionSmall studies; the message has to be true

These studies were small, with a few dozen students per group. And the message has to be true. It only works if you really do help students reach the standard.

For teaching

State your high standards plainly. Say that you believe each student can reach them. Give specific next steps, not just a grade. And build in time to revise.
