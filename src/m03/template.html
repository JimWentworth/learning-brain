Depth and meaning What you think about is what you keep

The same word is remembered far better after a question about its meaning than after a question about its letters. Trying to remember matters less than you might expect.

Three questions about the same word. Is it in capital letters? Does it rhyme with train? Would it fit the sentence “The student used her ___ to solve the problem”?

On a surprise test later, people in Fergus Craik and Endel Tulving’s 1975 study recognized about 1 in 7 of the words they had judged for capital letters, about half the rhyme words, and about 4 in 5 of the words they had fitted into a sentence. The same kinds of words appeared in every condition, and nobody was trying to memorize them. What people were asked to think about decided what stayed.

Levels of processingMeaning leaves a deeper trace

This is the levels-of-processing idea, proposed by Craik and Robert Lockhart in 1972: processing for meaning leaves a more durable trace than processing for surface features like letters or sound.

In the brain, a 1994 study by Shitij Kapur and colleagues scanned 12 people as they read nouns. When they decided whether each noun was living or non-living, the left inferior prefrontal cortex was more active than when they checked each word for the letter a, and they later recognized the living/non-living words far better. In 1998, Anthony Wagner and colleagues found that stronger left prefrontal and temporal activity while judging a word predicted whether it would be remembered.

IntentTrying to remember matters less than you’d think

When Craik and Tulving warned people about the test, memory for capital-letter words rose only to about a quarter, and meaning words stayed at about 80%. In earlier studies by Thomas Hyde and James Jenkins, rating words for pleasantness produced recall about as good as deliberately studying them. Counting the letter e in each word did not.

Not just timeDepth isn’t the same as effort

Meaning questions usually take a little longer. But in one of Craik and Tulving’s experiments, a slow, fiddly task about the pattern of consonants and vowels in each word took longer than an easy question about meaning, and still produced worse memory.

Two cautionsPartly circular, and not always best

First, depth has never had a clean measure apart from memory itself, so the idea is partly circular: deep processing is what leads to good memory, and good memory shows the processing was deep. Second, deep isn’t always best. In 1977, Donald Morris, John Bransford and Jeffery Franks showed that on a test about rhymes, studying for rhyme beat studying for meaning. What matters is the match between how something is learned and how it is used, which the transfer page takes further.

Look at what your activities make students think about

Highlighting and copying keep attention on the surface. Instead, ask for judgments about meaning: why does this step work? How is this case like the last one? Which example would break the rule? Have students sort, compare and explain, rather than transcribe. And be wary of tasks that are busy but shallow. Memory is a by-product of thinking.
