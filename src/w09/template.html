Writing to learn

Writing about a subject can deepen understanding of it, but not all writing does. Short tasks that make students explain, predict or monitor their own understanding do more than long reports or copying.

Run it in class

  1. Run the activity, voting on each task.
  2. Then try one: give students two minutes to write the muddiest point of today’s class and why it is unclear.
  3. Read a handful aloud (anonymously) and address the most common one.
  4. Ask students which kinds of writing have helped them learn in other courses.

Ask the room

  • What do the helpful prompts have in common?
  • Why might a long paper teach less of the content than several short prompts?
  • Which short writing prompt could you add to your next class?

Connect it to your course

Add short, frequent writing that asks students to explain, predict, or monitor their understanding: a muddiest point, a one-sentence summary of the key idea, a prediction before a demonstration. Read a sample and respond.

Good to know

Most of the evidence comes from school classrooms; studies in college courses are fewer and more mixed. Long papers can serve other goals, such as learning to write in the discipline.

In 2004, Robert Bangert-Drowns, Marlene Hurley and Barbara Wilkinson pooled 48 school-based writing-to-learn programmes. Writing had a small, positive effect on conventional measures of achievement. Two features predicted larger effects: prompts that asked students to reflect on their own understanding, and longer programmes. Two predicted smaller ones: middle-school grades, and longer writing assignments.

A 2020 meta-analysis by Steve Graham, Sharlene Kiuhara and Meade MacKay, covering 56 experiments in science, social studies and mathematics, found that writing about content reliably enhanced learning, with an average effect size of 0.30.

What kind of writingThinking, not transcribing

Writing to learn is writing that makes students do something with the content: explain it, connect it, apply it or notice what they don’t understand. Prompts asking students to reflect on their own understanding were the strongest predictor of benefit in the 2004 analysis. Longer assignments, perhaps because they shift effort toward producing the text itself, were linked to smaller effects.

Every subjectNot just for essay courses

Graham and colleagues found the benefit was about equal in science, social studies and mathematics, and across elementary, middle and high school. The individual studies varied widely, though: some found large gains and some found little, which suggests that the design of the task matters a great deal.

A cautionThin evidence in college

Both meta-analyses studied schools. Research in college courses is sparser, and some studies have found no measurable effect on achievement even when students valued the writing. Short, focused prompts are a low-cost bet; they are not a guarantee.

For language learnersWriting builds both

For students learning in a second language, short low-stakes writing gives practice using the discipline’s vocabulary without the pressure of a graded paper. Respond to the ideas first.
