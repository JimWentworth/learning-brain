Put on goggles fitted with wedge prisms and the world appears shifted to one side. In a 1996 study, T. A. Martin, Amy Bastian, W. T. Thach and colleagues asked people to throw balls of clay at a target while wearing them. At first, each throw missed in the direction of the shift. Throw by throw, people corrected, until they hit the target again.

Then the goggles came off, and people missed to the other side. Their aim had been recalibrated, and it took more throws to set it back. That aftereffect is the sign that something had been learned, not just consciously corrected. People with damage to the cerebellum, or to the pathways that feed it, adapted much less.

An error correctorCompare, then adjust

The cerebellum sits at the back of the head, below the rest of the brain. It does not decide what to do. It works out how to do it smoothly. As a movement unfolds, it compares what you meant to do with what your eyes and body report actually happened. The difference, an error signal, adjusts the next try.

That is why the prism study works as a test. Healthy throwers did not just aim differently on purpose. Their cerebellum quietly re-tuned the link between where they looked and where they threw, which is why the old setting came back as a miss once the goggles were removed.

The screen version has a difference you may have noticed. You could see the misses and choose to aim off, so much of your correction was a conscious strategy. In the real study, throwers corrected without deciding to, and most of the aftereffect came from that automatic recalibration. People with cerebellar damage could still use a strategy to reduce their misses, but they did not get back to their normal accuracy, and their aftereffect was much smaller.

TimingBlink just in time

The cerebellum also learns when to act. In eyeblink conditioning, a tone is followed by a puff of air to the eye. After many pairings, animals and people blink to the tone alone, timed to close the eye just before the puff arrives. Beginning in 1981, Richard Thompson and colleagues showed that this learning depends on the cerebellum. When parts of its cortex are switched off, blinks may still happen, but the precise timing is lost.

In 2009, Frederico Azevedo, Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues counted the cells in four whole human brains by grinding up the tissue and counting the cell nuclei. They found about 86 billion neurons in all, about 69 billion of them in the cerebellum and only about 19% in the much larger cerebral cortex. Most of the cerebellum’s neurons are tiny granule cells.

More than movement

Most of what is known about the cerebellum concerns movement, balance and timing. Researchers increasingly find links to thinking and language too, but those roles are less well understood. This page sticks to the well-established part.

Why it matters for learningErrors are the signal

  1. Quick, specific feedbackThe cerebellum learns from the gap between what you meant and what happened. Practice works best when learners can see that gap right away.
  2. Expect early missesMistakes in early practice are the information that drives correction, not a sign of failure.
  3. Unlearning takes practice tooA tuned skill doesn’t switch off instantly. Changing a well-practised technique means practising the new one until it takes over.
