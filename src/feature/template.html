The Test
That Teaches

We hold up a test like a ruler, to measure what was learned. The research says the act of answering is where much of the learning happens.

Ask a room of students how they prepare for an exam and most will describe the same ritual: highlighter in hand, they read the chapter again, and then again. The words grow familiar. Familiar feels like learned.

Roediger & Karpicke, 2006, Psychological Science. The figures below are the share of the passage's ideas students recalled on the final test.

In 2006 the psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke put that ritual to a simple test. College students read short science passages. Then half of them read the passage a second time, while the others put it away and wrote down everything they could remember, with no feedback on what they got right.

Then the researchers waited, and tested everyone again.

Five minutes after studying, rereading looked like the smarter strategy. A week later the picture had flipped. The students who had spent their time pulling the material out of memory kept far more of it than the students who had put it in a second time.

What makes the result unsettling is how it felt from the inside. Students who reread were more confident about how much they would remember, even though they ended up remembering less. Their own sense of learning was pointing the wrong way.

Rereading wins the sprint.Recall wins the week.

Why it worksFluency is not the same as a path back

Rereading makes a page feel familiar, and familiarity is a quick, cheap feeling. Recall is different work: starting from a question, the brain has to find its own way to the answer. Each successful search strengthens that route, and each failed search shows exactly what is missing, which a fluent reread hides.

Not a one-offHundreds of studies, the same direction

A single experiment can mislead. This one did not. In 2017 Olusola Adesope and colleagues pooled the research on practice tests and found them ahead of rereading and every other comparison they examined, in laboratories and in classrooms, for remembering facts and for using them in new situations.

Classrooms are the harder test: real grades, busy students, messy schedules. In 2021 Chunliang Yang, David Shanks and colleagues gathered classroom studies from primary school to university. Quizzing raised achievement by about half a standard deviation, large by the standards of education research, and the benefit held across school levels and subjects.

Won’t more tests mean more stress?

It is a kind worry, and the evidence mostly points the other way. A 2023 review by Yang and colleagues pooled 24 studies with more than 3,000 students that measured test anxiety before and after a stretch of regular quizzing. On average, anxiety went down by a medium amount. The benefit was largest when quizzes were easier and lower stakes.

The authors call this research young, and very anxious students may respond differently. But for most students, practice makes the real test familiar, and familiar is less frightening.

For teachingThree moves for next week

  1. Build in short practice testsEvery week, and call them practice. A few questions at the start or end of class is enough.
  2. Ask for recall, not recognitionQuestions that make students produce the answer, or multiple choice with wrong answers that are genuinely tempting.
  3. Explain the evidenceStudents will not choose testing on their own while rereading feels better. Show them the crossover.
