Fifty-four students wrote short essays over four months, in three groups: with ChatGPT, with a search engine, or with only their own heads. Electrodes recorded their brain activity (EEG) as they wrote. Nataliya Kosmyna and colleagues at the MIT Media Lab posted the results in 2025.

Brain connectivity scaled down with the amount of outside support. The unaided group showed the strongest, most widespread networks. Search users were in between. The AI group showed the weakest.

Memory and ownershipWritten, but not remembered

Minutes after finishing, most of the AI group could not correctly quote a sentence from the essay they had just written. In the first session the reported figure was about 83%, against about 11% in the other groups. They also felt the least ownership of their essays.

The swapUnaided first, then AI

In a fourth session, the groups swapped. Students who had first written unaided, and then used AI, showed higher engagement and used it more strategically. Students moving the other way showed weaker connectivity than those who had practised unaided all along.

GenerationWhere the thinking happens

This is the generation effect again. Writing is not only a way to show thinking. It is where much of the thinking happens. Hand it off, and the encoding goes with it.

A cautionA warning sign, not a verdict

This is a preprint with a small sample: 54 people, and only 18 in the swap session. EEG connectivity is an indirect measure. Treat it as a warning sign, not a verdict.

Protect an unaided stage

Have students draft their ideas and structure before any AI use. Ask them to explain or quote their own work afterwards. And when AI comes in, use it to critique and extend a draft the student already owns.
