In 2013, Karl Szpunar, Novall Khan and Daniel Schacter at Harvard had students watch a 21-minute statistics lecture online, in segments. Some students answered short quiz questions after each segment. Others did not, or simply restudied.

At random moments, students were asked whether their minds were wandering.

WhyA reason to stay with it

Knowing a question is coming gives students a reason to stay with the material. And answering is itself retrieval practice, which strengthens memory. It also shows students, and you, what has not been understood yet.

A cautionPromising, not final

This was one lecture on one topic, in a lab. The authors themselves noted it is not yet clear how well the benefit holds across a whole course, or in a live classroom. It is a promising finding, not a final answer.

For teaching

Break videos into short segments with one or two questions between them. Keep the questions low-stakes and quick. Use them in live lectures too, with polls or a minute of writing. And give quick feedback on the answers, so mistakes get corrected. Share this tip with students: pause and quiz yourself every few minutes.
