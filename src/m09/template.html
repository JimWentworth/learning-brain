Why we forget Mostly, the way back is lost

Forgetting is not just time passing. Much of it is interference from similar material, and much of what seems forgotten is still stored, just out of reach.

InterferenceSimilar things compete

Much forgetting is interference. In 1957, Benton Underwood looked across many lab studies and found that the more lists people had learned before, the more they forgot of the next one. With that interference removed, forgetting over a day fell from about 75% to about 25%. Similar material competes, and new learning can also crowd out the old.

Still thereAvailable, but not accessible

Much of what seems forgotten is still stored. As the first topic showed, people who couldn’t recall words freely recalled many more when given the category names.

Mice show this at the level of cells. In 2015, Tomás Ryan, Susumu Tonegawa and colleagues gave mice a drug that blocked consolidation of a fear memory. The mice could no longer recall the fear from natural cues. But when the engram cells were switched on with light, the memory came back. The trace was stored; the path to it was lost.

Forgetting helpsNot only failure

Losing access to outdated details, like an old address or last year’s timetable, keeps current ones easy to find. And in Robert and Elizabeth Bjork’s framework, a memory that has become harder to retrieve gains more from being retrieved again, which is one reason spacing works.

A cautionDepends on the material

Much of this comes from word lists and animal studies. How fast we forget depends on the material, how well it was learned, and how it’s tested.

When students go blank

Expect steep early loss, and revisit material within days. Separate similar topics, or teach their differences directly. And when students go blank, give a cue before giving the answer.
