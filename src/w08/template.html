Lectures in a second language

Following a lecture in a second language is a different task from following it in your first. A few unknown words per hundred can make the difference between understanding and losing the thread.

Read with gaps

Two short passages. In one, 2 words in every 100 are unknown to you. In the other, 10 in every 100. Read each once, then answer two questions without looking back.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Students read each passage once, at their normal pace, then answer the questions without scrolling back.
  2. Ask how the second passage felt. What did students do when they hit an unknown word?
  3. Explain that a lecture moves at the speaker’s pace: listeners can’t reread.
  4. Look at one of your own slides or a passage of your notes. Which words might be unknown to some students?

Ask the room

  • What did the unknown words cost you, beyond the words themselves?
  • How would this feel at lecture speed, with no chance to reread?
  • Which words in your course are likely unknown to students learning in a second language?

Connect it to your course

Give students the key terms before class, post slides or notes in advance, caption your recordings, and speak at a steady pace with pauses after key points. Each of these lets students prepare or recover the words they missed.

Good to know

Reading lets you pause and reread; listening doesn’t. If anything, this activity understates how hard a lecture with unknown words can be.

Researchers call the share of words in a text that a reader or listener knows lexical coverage. Most research suggests that coverage of 95–98% is needed for adequate comprehension, in other words, that only 2–5% of words can be unknown.

That sounds generous until you translate it. At 95% coverage, one word in every 20 is unknown; at 98%, one in every 50.

ReadingThe 98% figure

In a widely cited study, Marcella Hu and Paul Nation found that about 98% coverage was needed for adequate, unassisted reading comprehension of a relatively easy fiction text. Later work suggested two thresholds: 95% for minimal comprehension and 98% for good comprehension.

ListeningInformal talk versus lectures

Hilde van Zeeland and Norbert Schmitt found that 95% coverage may be enough to follow informal spoken narratives, which can be reached with 2,000–3,000 word families. Academic listening is a different matter: one study found that 98% coverage was needed for academic listening.

CaptionsSeeing the words helps

A 2013 meta-analysis by Maribel Montero Perez, Wim Van den Noortgate and Piet Desmet pooled studies of second-language learners watching video with captions in the same language. Captions had a large effect on listening comprehension (15 studies) and on vocabulary learning (10 studies).

For learningGive students a way to catch up

A lecture runs at the speaker’s pace. A reader can stop on an unknown word; a listener loses it, and often the next sentence too. Key terms in advance, slides and notes to review, and captioned recordings give students learning in a second language the chance to recover what they missed.
