You might think the students who get the most done simply have more willpower. The research points somewhere else. They lean less on willpower, and more on habits.

A habit is a behaviour set off by a cue, like a time or a place, rather than by a fresh decision. In a study at University College London, reported by Phillippa Lally and colleagues in 2010, 96 volunteers repeated one daily behaviour after a fixed cue, such as after breakfast, for 12 weeks.

Self-controlResisting less, not more

So why does self-control predict good outcomes? Across six studies with more than 2,000 people, Brian Galla and Angela Duckworth at the University of Pennsylvania found that people with better self-control reported resisting temptation less often, not more. Their habits, including study habits, explained much of the link between self-control and good outcomes, such as being able to study even when it was hard.

The fuel tankA shaky idea

You may have heard that willpower is a tank that runs dry. Earlier studies suggested a sizeable effect. But a replication across 23 labs and more than 2,000 people found an effect close to zero. The debate goes on, so planning your day around a fuel tank is a shaky strategy.

A cautionSmall study, correlations

The habit study was small, and about half the volunteers never showed a clear habit curve. The self-control findings are correlations. And more complex behaviours took longer to become automatic.

Try this

Pick one fixed time and place for study. Attach it to something you already do, like after lunch. Start small enough to manage on a bad day. And if you miss a day, simply start again tomorrow.
