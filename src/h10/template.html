Think of something you study because you want to, and something you study only because it is required. Psychologists call the first intrinsic motivation: doing something because it is interesting or meaningful to you.

Self-determination theory, developed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, says intrinsic motivation grows when three needs are met: autonomy (some say in what you do), competence (feeling yourself get better), and relatedness (feeling connected to others).

RewardsThey can backfire

A meta-analysis of 128 experiments, by Deci, Richard Koestner and Ryan, found that expected, tangible rewards for an interesting task reduced people’s later free choice to keep doing it. Positive feedback did the opposite, and raised interest.

ChoiceA few choices help

Across 41 studies, reviewed by Erika Patall, Harris Cooper and Jorgianne Civey Robinson, having choices increased intrinsic motivation, effort and performance. Effects were strongest with a few successive choices, around two to four, rather than one.

RelevanceWrite the connection

In high school science classes, Chris Hulleman and Judith Harackiewicz asked 262 ninth graders to write about how the material connected to their own lives. Students who had expected to do poorly became more interested in science and earned higher grades. Later studies found similar benefits in college courses, especially for lower-performing students.
