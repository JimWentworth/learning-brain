Retrieval practice Pull it out to make it stay

Rereading feels like learning because the page becomes familiar. Trying to recall the material feels harder, because you notice what’s missing. A week later, the recall group remembers more.

Two groups study the same short passage. One reads it four times. The other reads it once, then three times tries to write down everything it remembers, with no feedback. Five minutes later, the rereaders are ahead: 83% of the ideas recalled against 71%. A week later the result flips: 61% for the group that practised recall, 40% for the rereaders.

That was Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke’s 2006 experiment. The rereaders were also the more confident group.

Why it worksEach recall strengthens the route back

Rereading makes a text familiar, and familiarity feels like knowing. Recall is different work. Starting from a question, you have to find your own way to the answer, and each successful search strengthens that route for next time. When the search fails, you find out exactly what is missing, which a smooth reread hides.

The benefit is not limited to the lab. In 2017, Olusola Adesope and colleagues pooled 272 effects from 118 articles and found practice tests ahead of restudying, in classrooms as well as labs. In 2011, Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt found that retrieval practice beat drawing concept maps a week later, even on questions that asked students to draw inferences.

The puzzleWhy students rarely choose it

In 2009, Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger asked 177 college students how they studied. 84% said they reread their notes or textbook, and 55% named it as their main strategy. Only 11% said they practised recall, and 1% named it as their main strategy. Rereading feels productive. Recall feels hard, and the hard feeling is easy to mistake for not learning.

Some cautionFeedback, and low stakes

Feedback matters, especially after wrong answers, so errors don’t stick. In a 2005 study by Harold Pashler and colleagues, people learning foreign words who were shown the correct answer after an error remembered far more a week later than those who weren’t. And keep the stakes low: the point is practice, not grading.

Make pulling it out part of every lesson

Open class with two or three questions on last week’s material. Ask students to write down everything they remember before they look at their notes. Swap some review lectures for practice problems and quick quizzes, give feedback fast, and tell students why it feels hard.
