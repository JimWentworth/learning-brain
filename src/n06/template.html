In 1993, Frances Rauscher and colleagues reported in Nature that college students did better on a spatial reasoning task after ten minutes of a Mozart sonata. The boost was small, and it faded within about fifteen minutes. Rauscher stressed that the test measured only one kind of spatial ability.

The stretchFrom students to babies

Headlines turned a brief effect on one kind of task into: Mozart makes you smarter. And then: Mozart makes babies smarter, which the study never tested. By the late 1990s, more news stories about the effect mentioned babies than college students.

The evidenceHard to replicate

Other labs found the result hard to replicate. In 2010, Jakob Pietschnig and colleagues pooled nearly 40 studies with more than 3,000 people. Compared with silence or no stimulus, the effect was small. Compared with other music, it was smaller still. And studies by the original research group found noticeably larger effects than studies by others.

The grain of truthMood and alertness

A short burst of music you enjoy can lift mood and alertness for a while, which may help briefly. But that is not Mozart, and it is not intelligence. The bigger lesson is how a small, short-lived finding became a lasting belief, simply by being repeated.

For teaching

Treat music in class or study as a mood choice, not a cognitive enhancer. For students who study with lyrics, suggest trying silence or instrumental music for hard reading. Share this tip with students: play music you enjoy for mood, not for IQ.
