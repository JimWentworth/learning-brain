Where experts look, and pointing the way

Experts look at different places than beginners, and they get there faster. Students can’t see where your eyes go, but you can show them: point, highlight, and say where to look.

Ready

Find the T

Find the one T among the Ls and click it (or tab to it and press Enter). Some rounds give you a pointer first; some don’t. Eight rounds, timed.

About 10 minutes

Run it in class

  1. Students do the eight rounds on their own devices and note their two average times: with a pointer and without.
  2. Collect a few pairs of times. Was the pointer faster for nearly everyone?
  3. Project a diagram from your course. Ask students to mark where they would look first. Then show where an expert looks, and why.
  4. Discuss: what is the “T” in our diagrams, and how do I point to it?

Ask the room

  • How did you search when there was no pointer?
  • How much did the pointer save you?
  • Where do you look first in this kind of diagram, and how did you learn that?

Connect it to your course

When you show a complex image, point to the part that matters, highlight it, or build it up step by step, and say what you are looking at and why. Recording a short video with your cursor or pen showing where you look is a simple version of an expert’s eye-movement model.

Good to know

Search time depends on the screen and the person. The comparison that matters is each person’s own time with and without the pointer.

Andreas Gegenfurtner, Erno Lehtinen and Roger Säljö pooled 296 effect sizes from eye-tracking studies comparing experts and non-experts looking at visualisations in fields such as medicine, sport and aviation. Experts had shorter fixations, looked more at the task-relevant areas and less at irrelevant ones, and got their eyes to the relevant information sooner.

In other words, part of expertise is knowing where to look, and that is invisible to a learner sitting next to the expert.

SignallingPoint to what matters

Highlighting, arrows, colour cues and spoken pointers are called signalling in multimedia research. A 2018 meta-analysis by Sascha Schneider and colleagues, covering more than 12,000 learners, found that signals improved retention (g = 0.53) and transfer (g = 0.33). A 2020 meta-analysis by David Alpizar and colleagues also found a positive average effect (d = 0.38), with effects varying from small to large.

Showing the gazeEye-movement modelling examples

Researchers have tried showing learners an expert’s eye movements directly: a video in which a dot or spotlight marks where the expert is looking while they explain. In one study, Halszka Jarodzka and colleagues used this approach to teach medical students to recognise seizures in infants from patient videos. Results across studies are mixed, and some find no benefit, so this is a promising idea rather than a settled one. Simple signalling has the stronger evidence.

For learningYour eyes know; say where they go

When you look at a graph, a slide or a specimen, you go straight to what matters. Students can’t see that. Narrate it: “First I look at the axes. Then the gap between these two lines.” Point while you say it.
