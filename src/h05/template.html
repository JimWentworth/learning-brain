Exam week often brings sugary snacks, energy drinks and skipped meals. Your brain isn’t running out of fuel mid-problem set, but regular meals help, and caffeine has a catch.

Your brain is about 2% of your body weight, but uses roughly 20% of your energy at rest. Hard thinking adds only a little on top, perhaps around 5%. Your brain is not running out of fuel halfway through a problem set.

A popular idea held that self-control burns blood sugar, and a sugary drink tops it back up. But a 2016 analysis by Miguel Vadillo and colleagues found signs of publication bias in that research, and weak evidence overall. Experiments that measured people’s blood glucose directly found no link with self-control either.

MealsBreakfast helps that morning

Meals still matter. A review of 45 studies in children and teenagers, by Katie Adolphus and colleagues, found that breakfast, compared with fasting, gave a short-term boost that same morning, especially on tasks of attention and memory. It could not say which breakfast works best.

CaffeineAlertness, not memory

Caffeine is the most common study aid. Low to moderate doses, roughly 40 to 300 milligrams, improve alertness, attention and reaction time. Effects on memory and complex reasoning are less consistent.

The catch is sleep. In a study by Christopher Drake and colleagues, 400 mg of caffeine taken six hours before bed cut sleep by more than an hour, and people did not notice.

The question is always: what does the evidence actually show?

A cautionSmall studies, big differences

The breakfast studies are mostly in children. The caffeine and sleep study had just 12 people. And responses to caffeine vary a lot from person to person.

Try this

Eat at regular times, especially before morning exams. Don’t count on sugar for a willpower boost. Use caffeine early in the day, for alertness, not memory. And stop caffeine at least six hours before bed.
