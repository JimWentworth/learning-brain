Two hundred and forty-six people solved 20 logical-reasoning problems from the Law School Admission Test (LSAT), with an AI assistant. Their scores went up by about 3 points over a norm group. But they thought they had done about 4 points better than they actually did. Daniela Fernandes, Robin Welsch and colleagues reported this in 2026.

Normally on this kind of task, the weakest performers overestimate themselves the most. That is the Dunning–Kruger pattern. With AI, it disappeared: everyone overestimated, by about the same amount.

Know-howMore AI literacy, less self-accuracy

People who knew more about how AI works were more confident, but less accurate about their own performance. That is the opposite of what you might expect.

One prompt, no checkNo feedback, no correction

Most people sent a single prompt per question and accepted the answer. Without testing the reasoning, they got no feedback on where they were wrong. And people working with the AI did not beat the AI working alone.

MetacognitionFluent answers feel like understanding

This is the metacognition problem from Series 1, sharpened. Our feeling of knowing tracks how fluent something feels, not how accurate it is. A smooth, confident answer feels like understanding. Judging your own accuracy is a skill, and AI can quietly switch it off.

A cautionPuzzles, one model, online samples

These are reasoning puzzles, with one model, in online samples. But they fit a wider finding about fluency and the feeling of knowing.

Make reflection a routine

Before students see an AI answer, have them predict their confidence, then check. Ask them to explain the AI’s reasoning back, or find one flaw in it. On some tasks, grade calibration, not just correctness. And make it a routine, not a one-off.

Why it matters for learningAI can make students smarter on the task. Only reflection makes them wiser about it.
