Metacognition Feeling you know is not knowing

The feeling of learning and actual learning can come apart. Fluency, how easy something seems right now, drives the feeling. The fix is simple: wait, try to recall, and only then judge what you know.

FluencyEasy is not the same as learned

The feeling comes from fluency: how easy something seems right now. Big print is easy to read; a page you’ve just reread is easy to follow. As the retrieval practice page shows, most students study by rereading, which feels fluent.

StubbornThe illusion survives the evidence

In 2008, Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork had people learn the painting styles of 12 artists. Some artists’ paintings were shown in blocks, one artist at a time; others were mixed together. Mixing worked better for most people. Yet 78% rated blocking as better, even after their own test had shown the opposite.

The repairWait, then judge

Judge your learning right after studying, and you are reading short-term memory. Wait, try to recall, and only then judge, and predictions become far more accurate. This delayed judgment-of-learning effect, first reported by Thomas Nelson and John Dunlosky in 1991, is one of the most reliable findings in the field.

In the brain, Stephen Fleming and colleagues found in 2010 that people who were better at judging their own accuracy tended to have more grey matter in the anterior prefrontal cortex, at the very front. But this was a correlation in 32 people. It doesn’t show that the region causes the skill, or that the skill is fixed.

Teach students to check

Warn students that fluent is not the same as learned. Have them predict their score, then compare it with the result. Use delayed self-tests, not rereading, as the check. After exams, ask what they did and what they will change. And model it yourself: say when you are unsure, and how you would check.
