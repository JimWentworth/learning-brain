It is a hard question to test, because students and teachers are rarely matched at random. But Tennessee’s STAR experiment, in the 1980s, randomly assigned young students and their teachers to classrooms, from kindergarten through third grade. Years later, that made a rare fair test possible.

Economists Seth Gershenson, Cassandra Hart, Joshua Hyman, Constance Lindsay and Nicholas Papageorge followed up, and published the results in 2022.

+13%more likely to graduate from high school (9 points), for Black students with at least one Black teacher in K–3
+19%more likely to enroll in college (6 points)

Years later

Black students randomly assigned at least one Black teacher in kindergarten to third grade, compared with Black schoolmates who were not.

Where the gains wereConcentrated, and long-run

The gains were concentrated among disadvantaged Black boys, and the college gains came mostly from two-year colleges. Data from North Carolina showed a similar pattern. And these long-run effects were not visible in short-run test scores alone.

The researchers suggest role-model effects. A teacher who shares your background can change your beliefs about what is possible for someone like you.

Who do your students see?

The question is always: can more students see a future self here?

A cautionEarliest grades, one group

This is about the earliest grades, and one group of students. And most instructors cannot change who they are. But you can change who your students see.

For teaching

Invite speakers and alumni from varied backgrounds. Feature a range of people in your examples and readings. And connect students with mentors and peers they can identify with. Look beyond test scores when you judge whether it is working.
